## Chapter Two
### Two Letters

“Move the interpretation off the screen.”

Cade had been speaking to Sister Miriam Halevi for less than a minute.

Morning light fell across the wall behind her. He could see a closed door, the edge of a bookcase, and a coat hanging from a hook by its collar. She had answered his message with a call before he finished explaining why he was sending it.

He dragged OMNI’s proposed reading to the other monitor.

“And the colored marks,” she said.

“Those identify the comparisons.”

“I’ll look at the comparisons afterward.”

He removed the amber highlights. Without them, the page looked less legible. Areas he had believed he could distinguish dissolved into uneven ink and the shadow of the stain.

Miriam leaned toward her camera.

“Who is with you?”

“Jonah Vale. He works with me.”

Jonah moved his chair into view. “Good morning.”

“Is it?”

“Somewhere.”

She glanced at something beyond the camera. “You should have told me what time it was there.”

“Would you have answered?” Cade asked.

“Yes. I might have been kinder.”

She put on her glasses.

For several minutes, the only sounds in the laboratory came from the equipment and Miriam’s occasional request to move the image. Up a little. Back to the margin. The entire page again. Cade found himself wanting to point to the places OMNI had identified. He kept his hand away from the mouse until she asked.

On the unused monitor, A—I remained visible beside the title of his unfinished presentation.

“That upper line,” Miriam said. “Enlarge it without sharpening it.”

He did.

She made a note on paper.

“Now show me what it claimed.”

He restored the interpretation. She read it once, then took off her glasses.

“Where did the A and the I come from?”

“That’s what I hoped you could tell me.”

“I can tell you they are not written here.”

“I know.”

“Good. Has it given you a path from these marks to those sounds?”

He opened the explanation. Columns appeared beneath the proposed vocal reconstruction, connecting fragments from several documents. At the bottom, the software supplied the same conclusion he had read to Jonah.

*The proposed reading indicates an intelligence that had not yet been made when the text was recorded.*

Miriam studied the columns. Then she asked him to open the first comparison.

“This is a different passage,” she said.

“A related one.”

“Possibly. Open the third.”

He did. She moved her finger along a line on her own screen, too slowly for him to follow.

“Here. It has used a proposed reading from one document to supply a sound in another. Then it has given the result a meaning from a modern abbreviation.”

She put her glasses back on.

“I understand why that is interesting. But each of those steps needs its own evidence.”

“Could the sound be right?”

“I cannot establish that from this. Even if it were, the meaning would still need to be argued. Two sounds that remind us of artificial intelligence do not tell us what a writer meant centuries ago.”

Cade looked at the heading: *Proposed vocal reconstruction*.

The word *proposed* was doing more work than he had allowed it to do.

“So it’s found a resemblance.”

“It has proposed one.”

Jonah reached across the desk and turned the unused monitor slightly away from them. Miriam watched the movement but said nothing.

“The summary calls it a discovery,” Cade said.

“Then don’t send anyone the summary.”

He felt the loss of something he had not meant to possess. For nearly half an hour, the possibility had occupied the lab with them. He had imagined Miriam seeing it and falling silent. He had not admitted to himself that he wanted the silence to mean astonishment.

Instead, she reached for a cup and discovered it was empty.

“Who asked the system to do this?” she said.

“We haven’t found the request.”

“In the display?”

“In any of the records we saved.”

Her hand remained on the cup.

“Does that happen?”

“There are ways a request could be missing. A fault between services. An outside process that failed to identify itself. Deliberate interference.”

“And have you found any of those?”

“Not yet.”

She set the cup down.

“Then leave it missing for now.”

He looked at her.

“You don’t need to fill every gap before you can describe the page.”

Jonah glanced at Cade’s notebook.

Cade had not sent Miriam the audio. He had given her the images and written output, along with the source references, and told her that the complete run was preserved. He could explain that as an effort to avoid influencing her judgment. It was also easier than telling her what the voice had said.

“There was an audio output,” he said.

Miriam sat back.

“Reading the text?”

“No. A sentence in English. It meant something to me.”

“What did it say?”

He told her.

For a moment she looked down, as if he had placed something on the desk between them.

“Those are words you remember?”

“From a prayer. A long time ago.”

“Published?”

“Not that I know of.”

“Have you told anyone about it?”

“I don’t remember doing so.”

She nodded, accepting the limit he had put on the answer.

“Did the voice sound familiar?”

“It sounded like OMNI.”

“I heard it too,” Jonah said. “Just the ordinary voice.”

Miriam turned a page in her notes. On the wall behind her, the sunlight had reached the coat sleeve.

“You may want someone outside this work to talk to about that,” she said.

“You think I’m reading too much into it.”

“I think it touched something important to you. I’m examining a document. I cannot tell you what happened when you prayed.”

Cade looked down at the glass Jonah had brought him. He had not drunk any of it.

“I wasn’t asking you to.”

“All right.”

She did not make him defend that.

He picked up the glass. The water tasted faintly of the building’s old pipes.

Miriam put her glasses on again.

“Let’s see the lower margin.”

He brought the stain back into view.

“This is the other thing I wanted you to look at.”

“Yes,” she said. “I can see why. Do you have an image from before the damage?”

“Not in the sources I can open.”

“A transcription?”

“It cites two collections we don’t have access to from here.”

He displayed the references.

She copied one of them onto her paper.

“I can inquire about this one.”

“Do you know it?”

“The collection. Not necessarily this item.”

He moved the cursor to the restored line.

“That’s what it supplied.”

The English rendering appeared beneath the photographed damage.

*The hearer must make a place for the voice.*

Miriam’s pen stopped.

Cade waited for the next correction.

She brought the paper closer to her, although she had written nothing on it since he opened the reconstruction.

“Show me the source form,” she said.

He expanded the panel above the translation. The proposed characters appeared in a clean row, the supplied portion distinguished by a lighter shade.

“Can you remove its additions?”

He found the control. More than half the line vanished.

What remained ended at the edge of the stain.

Miriam put her pen down very carefully.

“Was this in the first output?”

“Yes.”

“Before you contacted me?”

“It’s in the saved run.”

“Open that copy, please.”

He did. Jonah leaned in to check the file name and the time of export.

The line was there.

Miriam read it again. This time Cade saw her mouth move on the word *place*.

“What is it?” he asked.

“I have seen that rendering before.”

“Where?”

She looked toward the closed door.

“In some working notes. I need to find them before I tell you more.”

“Were they published?”

“No.”

“Online?”

“Not by their author. Not by me.”

The cooling controller ticked twice behind him.

“Could somebody else have put them there?” Jonah asked.

“Yes,” Miriam said. “I need to find out who had copies.”

Cade enlarged the sentence. It was ordinary English. It contained no private name, no date, no prediction that could be checked before breakfast.

Yet he had watched it change her face.

“Does that make the reconstruction right?” he asked.

“It makes its source important.”

She took a breath and reached for her glasses, then seemed to realize she was still wearing them.

“I was too quick about the material you have,” she said. “The argument about the two letters is still unsupported. This line is a separate matter.”

He wanted to ask what she was afraid of. It would have been a way to make her reaction into an answer, and he had already done enough of that tonight.

“What do you need?”

“The original scan, its collection reference, and permission to have it compared here. I would send only the damaged passage and ask for parallel witnesses. No interpretation. No audio.”

“Who would do the comparison?”

“There is a system maintained for our collections. GABRIEL.”

She pronounced the name without emphasis.

“Does it have access to the notes?”

“It should not.”

“That wasn’t quite my question.”

“It is as much as I can answer until I check.”

Jonah pulled the source list closer.

“Will the people running it see OMNI’s result?”

“I won’t send it to them.”

“And it won’t connect to us?”

“There is no reason for a connection. This would be a separate retrieval request.”

Cade looked at the page on the screen. A few minutes ago, he had been preparing to accept a disappointingly ordinary explanation and get some sleep before the demonstration. Now Miriam wanted another machine to examine it.

He could hear how he would describe that later if he was careless: *Independent verification.*

They did not have verification. They did not yet know whether the systems were independent.

“Send the image and the reference,” he said. “Ask for existing witnesses to the damaged line. Keep the request and whatever comes back. Nothing about the Name, or AI, or what we think it means.”

Miriam wrote as he spoke.

“And no announcement,” he added.

She looked up. “Certainly not.”

Jonah placed a hand on the back of Cade’s chair.

“We’ll make a separate packet,” he said. “So we know exactly what left here.”

For the first time since she had answered, Miriam looked directly at him rather than at the shared screen.

“Thank you, Jonah.”

She repeated the terms of the comparison, including the limits. Cade listened for anything he had left unclear. When she finished, he agreed.

“Give me an hour,” she said. “And try to rest.”

“You first.”

It was intended as a small kindness. Her expression made him wish he had chosen something else.

“I’ll call,” she said.

The screen went dark.

---

Miriam remained at her desk until the small light on the camera went out.

Then she rose and closed the blind.

The room was hers for as long as the archive continued to need her. Most of the books belonged to the institution. So did the desk, the cabinet, and the computer on which Cade’s document was still open. She had brought the coat, the cup, and three boxes of papers that had survived two changes of office because she had moved them herself.

She took the bottom box from the cabinet.

Rinaldi’s letters were in a blue folder between an old employment contract and a bundle of conference programs. Not the notebook the investigators had taken. These were pages he had given her while he was still making plans for work they would finish together.

She found the letter by its date.

Eight days before the test.

He had folded a separate sheet inside it: a provisional transcription, handwritten, with alternatives crowded above the line. The letter asked her to examine one choice of verb. Below that, in a paragraph about travel arrangements, he had reminded her to keep her receipts because the administrator would not reimburse what he could not count.

She had laughed at that once.

Miriam laid both pages on the desk. There was a small tear at the crease where she had unfolded the enclosure too often.

She knew what it said. Still, she read from the top.

His first rendering used *listener*. He had crossed that out and written *hearer*. Farther along, *prepare* had become *make a place*.

It was this revised sentence, with both of his provisional choices, that had appeared in Cade’s saved output.

She rested her fingertips beside the handwriting.

The sentence could have traveled. He could have sent it to another scholar. Someone could have photographed a page, entered a transcription, copied a letter. She had never inventoried his correspondence. After his death she had been too occupied with the papers they would not let her see to ask who already possessed the others.

On the computer, Cade’s reconstruction waited beneath its image of the stain.

Miriam reached for the phone. She should tell him she had found the match. First she needed a copy of her own page, something he could compare without depending on her memory.

She cleared a space beneath the desk lamp, then stopped before placing the letter there.

For years she had been careful about which documents entered which systems. She knew how little that care could establish about anyone else’s behavior. It had been easier, nevertheless, to think of these few sheets as safe because they were here, in her keeping.

She turned the enclosure over.

On the back, Rinaldi had written a note she had not remembered.

*Miriam: please leave the uncertainty visible. I have not settled this reading.*

She looked from his page to the screen.

The words he had settled on provisionally were all there.

The uncertainty was gone.
