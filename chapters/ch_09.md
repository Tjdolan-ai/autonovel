## Chapter Nine
### The Added S

“Which surviving mark gives you the plural?” Miriam asked.

Jonah looked up from the laptop.

“Put that in,” she said. “Exactly.”

The sentence remained on the screen while he typed beneath it.

*The WordS became flesh.*

Cade had first taken the capital at the end for an error in the display. He opened the saved response. The letter was there too.

“It came back that way,” Jonah said.

OMNI’s reply appeared below the question.

*The plural is supplied from the parallel tradition. It is not preserved as a complete form in the submitted image.*

Miriam drew the photographic plate closer.

“Then we begin with that.”

She pointed to the broken lower edge of the manuscript, then to its reproduction in the 1932 bulletin. The paper was missing in both.

“The S is English. It is part of this system’s rendering. We have not recovered another letter from the leaf.”

“Could it still be the right restoration?” Lucia asked.

“Possibly. So could the editor’s singular. We need the grounds for choosing.”

Marcus had not moved since the line appeared.

Cade asked OMNI to explain the phrase *parallel tradition*.

The reply occupied most of the screen. There were numbered references, short translated extracts, and an explanation connecting them. Cade began reading the explanation. Miriam went directly to the references.

“Open the first.”

Jonah did.

It led to an extract about creation through speech. The second cited a discussion of the Word. The third was labeled as a comparison of the two.

Miriam took a fresh sheet and wrote three numbers down its left side.

“Different texts,” she said. “Different settings. Who makes the connection?”

Cade read the next paragraph aloud.

“‘The familiar singular reflects a later personal interpretation of a more fundamental plurality.’”

He stopped.

That was a larger claim than the one he had asked the system to explain.

Miriam looked at him rather than at the screen.

“In the sentence we recognize, the Word is someone. This turns the subject into words—things that can be assembled, copied, transmitted.”

She put her pencil down.

“A translation has to account for that change.”

“Which text has the earlier plural?” Miriam asked. “What dates it? How did that reading reach this copy?”

Jonah entered the questions separately.

Each answer returned them to the commentary. None supplied the underlying document.

Marcus shifted in his chair.

“You have your answer.”

“I have an unsupported restoration,” Miriam said. “That isn’t the same as establishing what the page originally said.”

“You would leave this open?”

She glanced at the familiar sentence printed in the old bulletin.

“I believe that sentence. I still have to tell you whether this page contains it.”

Cade moved his notebook in front of the lower part of the screen. Without the new rendering beside the photograph, he could see the damaged edge more easily.

---

Jonah opened the source details rather than the displayed titles.

“These two are the same entry.”

He arranged the windows side by side.

The first title described a commentary on divine speech. The second described a note on the disputed passage. Underneath, both named the same source and section.

“And the comparison?” Cade asked.

“It cites that section.”

Miriam crossed out none of her three numbers. She drew lines from them to a single reference at the bottom of the sheet.

“Three routes to one reading.”

Lucia leaned over the sheet. “One reading can still be right.”

“Yes,” Miriam said. She left all three lines visible.

Cade compared the reference with the one Miriam had copied from the 1932 editor’s note. The names corresponded, though the system gave a more precise section than the bulletin did.

The singular completion and the plural argument both pointed toward a text they could not inspect.

“Does OMNI have the commentary?” he asked.

Jonah requested the underlying passage.

The system returned an English excerpt attributed to it. It supplied no image, no dated copy, and no information identifying the translator.

Lucia checked the catalog entry again.

“I can’t connect this wording to a document in the packet.”

Miriam wrote *unverified rendering* beside the excerpt.

“Keep the wording,” she told Jonah. “And keep the questions it did not answer.”

The excerpt was less concerned with the damaged leaf than Cade expected. It described meaning distributed through several incomplete witnesses, the pattern supposedly recoverable even when each witness lacked part of it. A later note applied the argument to reconstruction by machines.

“Later how?” Miriam asked.

The source information did not say.

Cade scrolled farther.

There was a proposed demonstration attached to the explanation.

He read it twice.

“This part makes a prediction.”

Marcus looked toward the door.

“Of course it does.”

---

They stopped when Lucia said the manuscript had been under the lamp long enough for the scheduled session.

Miriam finished the line she was transcribing. Lucia checked the leaves, lowered the protective cover, and took the cradle back through the staff door.

The investigation now occupied photographs, handwritten notes, and two laptops. Removing the original made the table look much less crowded.

Jonah rubbed his eyes.

“Have you eaten?” Miriam asked him.

“On the way in.”

“Coffee?”

“There may have been something beside it.”

She went to her bag and brought out a small packet of crackers. He accepted it without further argument and passed half to Cade.

Cade ate while reading the prediction again.

The system’s account claimed that a particular reconstruction method could recover exact missing language from partial copies, rather than merely propose likely wording. The stated conditions required more than one copy, a minimum amount of surviving text, and a limited length of loss. It also named a service unavailable through their present connection.

“We have enough to test a narrow version,” he said.

Miriam sat down.

“You mean finding words in a damaged text.”

“Recovering known words from a text we damage ourselves.”

“Those aren’t interchangeable.”

“No. But if it fails where we can check the answer, that matters when it asks us to trust an answer we can’t check.”

He turned his notebook so she could read it.

On one side he had written *Claim*. On the other, *What would count against it?*

“We choose complete passages. Make the incomplete copies after the method and its limits are fixed. Hold the originals back. It commits to its reconstruction before we uncover them.”

Jonah finished a cracker and leaned over the notebook.

“Is the missing part still there in one of the other copies?”

“The prediction includes a short span missing from every supplied copy,” Cade said. “It still claims the exact wording.”

Jonah read the relevant sentence on the screen before continuing.

“How much can be missing?”

“It has to specify that first.”

“And if it gives us a sentence that means roughly the same thing?”

“A failure. The claim is exact recovery.”

“What if it has already seen the complete text?” Miriam asked.

“Then we haven’t established recovery from the fragments. Some of the controls can be new passages we write ourselves, provided they meet its stated conditions.”

Jonah took the pencil Cade offered him.

“We keep every run. No choosing the best answer afterward.”

He added a line beneath the proposed comparison.

“And we need to know what the service is allowed to consult. Otherwise we’re measuring whatever somebody put behind the curtain.”

Cade nodded.

Miriam read the page without touching it.

“Suppose it succeeds.”

“Then we have a reconstruction result to explain.”

“Do we have evidence that words become flesh?”

“No.”

“That the plural belongs in this manuscript?”

“Not from this test.”

She looked at him.

“I need you to keep saying that when we’re somewhere more impressive than this table.”

---

Marcus remained standing after Lucia returned.

“There are people who can investigate the copying history without asking the system to perform.”

“We are investigating it,” Lucia said. She put the reproduction register beside her notes. “The request for the 1996 authorization is already with the records office.”

“Then let that work continue.”

Cade closed the source window, leaving the proposed comparison visible.

He could let someone else do it. For a moment the possibility felt like relief: hand over the files and go home with the mystery still intact.

Then he pictured returning to his laboratory with another folder of claims nobody could settle.

He wanted to bring back something useful. Something he could put on a table and say he had done properly.

“A reconstruction method could help preserve what a person actually wrote,” he said. “We’ve spent two days looking at what happens when other people supply the missing parts.”

“Including your system,” Marcus said.

“Yes.”

The word hurt more than Cade had expected.

“That is why I want an answer it can get wrong.”

Marcus studied him.

“And if it does?”

“The failure stays in the report.”

“The last report lost your objections.”

Jonah tapped his notebook.

“We still have them.”

Marcus looked at the page where Jonah had written the recording requirements. Then he picked up his case.

“Keep your own copy of everything.”

“We will,” Cade said.

Marcus left the copy of the signed reading permission with Lucia. His objection to a further test did not go with him; Miriam added it to their notes before she put her pencil down.

---

Cade opened the public correction. Jonah saw the heading and put down his bag.

“Do you want me to send it?”

“I’ll do it.”

Cade dialed Kestrel. Jonah stayed beside the open draft.

Kestrel answered on the second call.

Cade kept the conversation on speaker with Jonah’s recorder beside the phone. He described the reconstruction claim, the named service, and the limits they wanted to impose.

Kestrel asked for the service identifier. Cade sent that and the proposed conditions, without the testimony or its images.

There was a brief silence while Kestrel read.

“That service belongs to one of our research partners,” he said. “They operate it at the facility outside the city. I can arrange access there.”

“Why there?” Jonah asked.

“The licensed system runs on their equipment. They aren’t going to distribute the installation because I ask them to. But they can give you an engineering session.”

“Including the source and traffic records for our runs?”

“Tell them exactly what you need before accepting the setup.”

Cade looked at Miriam. She was writing down Kestrel’s words.

“The conference-room investigation remains separate,” Cade said. “This doesn’t clear that equipment or settle the missing recording.”

“Understood. Facilities is still working through the controller records.”

“And we haven’t accepted the transcript.”

“I received the transcript correction.”

Kestrel’s voice remained even. He had not tried to persuade them that the room had performed a miracle. Cade found it easier to talk to him because of that.

“What would this session require?”

“You bring the test design. They provide the installation and staff. Start by inspecting it. If the conditions can’t be met, don’t run it.”

“We select the passages after their configuration is fixed,” Jonah said.

“Put that in the request.”

“No publicity,” Miriam said. “No suggestion that attendance endorses the interpretation.”

“This is a research visit.”

She waited.

“I’ll put that in writing,” Kestrel added.

Cade returned to the service description. It referred to the same commentary as the linguistic explanation, this time through a technical note appended to the rendering.

“There’s another condition,” he said. “We need the text being cited. A document we can examine, with whatever information you have about where the rendering came from.”

“Send me the reference.”

“Before we run anything.”

“I’ll ask what they can supply.”

Cade sent the reference. Miriam checked it against her notebook before he did.

The call ended with a proposed time for inspection, not a start time for the test.

---

Jonah packed the independent recorder first. He left the notebook open beside Cade.

“We can refuse their setup,” he said.

“I know.”

“I mean when we’re there. After they’ve brought people in and cleared a room and told us what it cost.”

Cade looked up.

“You tell me it can’t be checked, we don’t run it.”

“Even if you think I’m wrong?” Jonah asked.

Cade looked down at the proposed conditions. “We stop first.”

Jonah nodded and closed the notebook.

Lucia asked for the inspection date so she could coordinate the outstanding document requests. Miriam gathered the two versions of the disputed sentence and placed the source references between them.

“Will you come?” Cade asked her.

“To inspect it. You made it possible to record a failure.”

She closed the folder.

“That is what I am agreeing to.”

The visit form arrived while they were still at the desk. Lucia printed it. It named the partnership facility, the proposed inspection time, and the documents Cade had requested. Beneath the conditions was a field for the lead investigator’s acknowledgment.

Cade read all of it.

Kestrel had included the prohibition on publicity. He had also written *Reconstruction Demonstration* at the top.

Cade crossed out *Demonstration* and wrote *Test* above it.

Then he signed beneath both.
