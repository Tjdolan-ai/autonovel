## Chapter Seven
### Those Who Keep the Silence

Miriam had almost reached the archive desk when the man beside it said her name.

She stopped. Cade, following her through the doorway, had to turn his shoulder to keep from touching the folder under her arm.

“Brother Marcus.”

The man wore a gray coat over a dark shirt. A pair of reading glasses hung from one hand. He looked first at Miriam, then at the transparent sleeve protruding from her folder.

Inside it was a print of R217.

“I need the photographs,” he said.

Miriam put the folder on the desk without opening it.

“Lucia authorized them.”

“I know.”

He laid a sheet beside her hand. Cade recognized the consultation number at the top. Beneath it were the same three item numbers Jonah had photographed the previous day.

“All three items?” Miriam asked.

“The images relating to this consultation. And an account of where copies have gone.”

Jonah came in behind Cade and let the door close.

“Copies of the photographs,” he said. “Or copies of our work?”

Marcus looked at him.

“The collection material.”

“Then put that on the request.”

The man adjusted his glasses but did not put them on.

They had spent the last half hour preparing a correction to the session report. Jonah had preserved the delivered file and its accompanying message. Miriam had attached photographs of the cards. Cade had written that their agreement to consider another session did not confirm anyone’s interpretation of the utterance. The conditions on a further test still applied.

He had sent that correction to the records service and everyone who had attended.

The reply was an acknowledgment of receipt.

Now another man wanted their account of what they had done, while offering very little account of himself.

“Who are you?” Cade asked.

“Brother Marcus has responsibilities for the restricted collection,” Miriam said.

“Which responsibilities?”

Marcus put the glasses on.

“I review uses that fall outside ordinary consultation. I have asked for the material to be withdrawn from further experiments.”

“Asked whom?”

“The cardinal’s office. And now you.”

Miriam glanced toward the staff door.

“Where is Lucia?”

“Coming.” Marcus lowered his voice. “We should speak first.”

“We are speaking.”

His eyes moved toward Cade and Jonah.

Miriam kept her hand on the folder.

“They’re already involved.”

---

Lucia arrived carrying a shallow tray of catalog slips. She saw Marcus’s paper and set the tray down carefully.

“Did you send that through my office?”

“I sent it to the office responsible for the decision.”

“Then it isn’t a decision yet.”

She read the sheet. Her expression changed at the second paragraph.

“You want their existing copies surrendered.”

“Until the review is complete.”

“I gave permission for the images.”

“Permission for examination.”

“Which is what they are doing.”

Marcus looked at Cade.

“Did the machines speak the text aloud?”

“Yes.”

“Was that necessary to establish the surviving letters?”

“It was a separate comparison.”

“Then we agree it was a separate use.”

Cade pulled out a chair. He had been standing longer than he intended, with his bag pulling against one shoulder.

“If there was a restriction we should have known about, show it to us.”

Lucia opened the consultation folder to the permission sheet she had signed.

Marcus took another page from his case.

The two documents lay side by side. One permitted photography for the approved comparison. The other required a further review before material from the restricted group was used in an experimental procedure. There was no reference from the first sheet to the second.

“This wasn’t in our packet,” Cade said.

“It should have been.”

“Whose job was that?” Jonah asked.

Marcus looked down at the papers.

“I will establish that.”

“You can include it in your report.”

Lucia drew a chair out for Miriam. Its back caught on the edge of the rug; she lifted it clear before sitting herself.

“The reading permission still stands,” she said. “Until someone with authority withdraws it.”

“And if something happens before they finish considering the wording?” Marcus asked.

“Something has happened. They need the records.”

He took off his glasses again.

“They need to stop.”

---

Miriam slid the disputed transcript toward him.

“This came from today’s session.”

Marcus read the first page, then the second. At the end he looked back at the paragraph above the final field.

“Is that what you said?”

“No.”

She placed a photograph of her card beside it.

He read that too.

“Who prepared this?”

“That is one of the things we’ve asked.”

His thumb rested along the edge of the printout. Cade watched for recognition and saw only attention.

“Then don’t let them use it,” Marcus said.

“We have challenged it. We also need the source records.”

“You have enough to know the report is unreliable.”

“Enough to challenge this report,” Miriam said. “Not enough to understand what has been happening.”

Marcus turned to Cade.

“How much have you been told about the earlier work?”

“A man died. We have the official summary and equipment findings. His own notebook is being withheld.”

“Matteo was my friend.”

Miriam looked down at her hands.

For a moment Marcus seemed about to say something to her. Instead he squared the two pages before him.

“That is why I am asking you not to do this again.”

“Then help us understand what happened to him,” Cade said.

“I do not hold the incident file.”

“What do you hold?”

The room was quiet enough for Cade to hear a printer starting behind the staff door.

Marcus put his glasses into their case.

“Accounts from other readers. Different periods. Different places. Some believed they were being addressed by the same speaker described in earlier records.”

“Believed,” Miriam said.

“Yes.”

“How could they compare?” Cade asked.

“Words. Repeated claims. What the speaker knew about them.”

“Had they read the earlier accounts?”

“Some had.”

“And the others?”

“I cannot establish what every person had seen.”

He said it without irritation. Cade had expected him to defend the stronger claim.

“Then why the certainty about stopping?”

Marcus looked at the photograph of Miriam’s card.

“Because I would rather answer for postponing a test than for letting somebody sit through another one.”

“You haven’t only postponed tests,” Lucia said. “You’ve postponed reading.”

He turned toward her.

“Those files contain things people did not tell their families.”

“Then begin with the portions we can examine without exposing them.”

“A passage removed from its account can mislead as easily as a passage removed from this leaf.”

“Then let us examine the account.”

Marcus looked tired. It was the first thing about him Cade had recognized without having to decide what it meant.

“Not today,” he said.

---

Lucia went to the tray she had brought in and selected a sleeve. There were two catalog cards inside it, one larger than the other.

Marcus reached toward them.

She left them on the desk, beyond his hand.

“These are finding aids. They are part of the consultation record.”

“The references lead into the restricted accounts.”

“Yes. That is what references are for.”

Miriam drew the sleeve closer but did not open it.

“I need to know which collection we are discussing.”

Marcus answered her.

“The Order of the Silent Word.”

Cade waited. The name seemed to mean something to everyone at the table except him and Jonah.

“A religious order?”

“A body of custodians,” Marcus said. “The name has been used in more than one arrangement.”

“For how long?”

“Our tradition places the beginning among those dispersed after the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem. The charge was to preserve accounts that should not be put to common use.”

Miriam looked up.

“Which is the earliest surviving document that says that?”

“The tradition is older than the copies we possess.”

“That may be. What can we date?”

He considered her for a moment.

“Not the founding.”

She nodded and wrote something in her notebook.

“Then we start with the copies.”

“If you are allowed to see them,” Jonah said.

“Yes.” Miriam kept writing. “That would help.”

Lucia slid the second card alongside the first. Near its upper corner was the stamped year 1968.

“This catalog entry describes an older account.”

Cade leaned forward. Much of the entry was in Italian. A short note in lighter pencil ran down one margin.

Marcus glanced at the card, then at Lucia.

“You know how little that gives them.”

“It gives them a place to begin.”

“And tomorrow they will put it into the machine.”

“Tomorrow we could put it on this table.”

She said it quietly enough that he stopped to hear her.

“The surviving copy with its wrapper. The descriptions that belong to it. No missing pages silently filled in. I can supervise the handling.”

“You want them to decide whether to continue.”

“Yes.”

“You have already decided.”

Lucia closed the empty tray.

“I have decided they should be able to read.”

Marcus shook his head.

“That is always where the Revelators begin.”

Cade looked at her.

“Revelators?”

“Some of us believe there may be a reason these accounts keep becoming relevant,” she said. “A reason beyond finding a better lock.”

“You believe this could be a revelation.”

“I believe the question is worth examining.”

“And he doesn’t.”

“He is a Guardian. He believes keeping the charge means keeping the material out of use.”

“It means preserving the reader too,” Marcus said.

Lucia’s expression changed.

“Did Matteo see these warnings?”

Marcus did not answer immediately.

Miriam stopped writing.

“Which warnings?”

Lucia looked toward the card.

“The references attached to the older accounts.”

“Did he see them?” Miriam asked Marcus.

“He saw summaries.”

“The accounts themselves?”

“His request was still under consideration.”

Miriam placed her pencil on the desk.

Cade thought of her waiting upstairs for a meal that had not happened. A request could remain under consideration while the person who made it ceased to exist.

“You should have told me,” she said.

Marcus kept his gaze on her.

“Yes.”

His answer did not open the file.

---

Lucia took a fresh request form from the drawer.

“One account,” she said to Marcus. “A supervised examination. You can identify any material that requires separate protection and give a reason. I will record the omissions.”

“You cannot promise them access before the review.”

“I am asking you to make a decision about an actual document.”

He looked at the blank form.

“Leave the reference. I will consider it.”

She entered the catalog number and placed the form where he could reach it.

Cade looked at the paper Marcus had brought.

“We aren’t surrendering our copies.”

Marcus’s face tightened.

“I am trying to prevent another use.”

“Then request a restriction on use. Don’t ask us to remove the evidence.”

“Your copies are not the only copies.”

“Then removing ours won’t contain it.”

Marcus opened his mouth, closed it, and looked at Jonah.

“Where are they?”

“Preserved,” Jonah said. “I can give you a list of the collection images. I’m not giving you control of our records.”

Cade felt a brief satisfaction at the answer. Then he remembered the correction he had not sent to the people outside this room.

He had found it much easier to demand that another man stop withholding things.

Miriam drew Marcus’s request toward her.

“Put your name on it. Your office, the scope of what you want, and the restriction you say applies. We will keep it with the other requests.”

He took out a pen.

While he wrote, Lucia asked Cade whether she could keep their written challenge to the session report with the consultation record.

“Yes.”

“With the witness-card photographs?”

“All of it.”

She fastened the pages together without changing their order.

Marcus finished writing and took a small self-inking stamp from his case. The rectangle it left beside his signature carried an inventory heading and a boxed 04. He pushed the request back, then sat still with the pen uncapped in his hand.

“Dr. Cade.”

Cade looked up.

“Has it spoken to you about something you remember from your own life? Something you believe no one else could know?”

Miriam’s hand stopped over the folder.

Cade felt the table beneath his palms. It was perfectly still.

“Why do you ask?”

“Because that has been reported before.”

“By whom?”

Marcus looked at the catalog sleeve.

Miriam turned it toward Cade. Her finger stopped beside the lighter pencil in the margin.

“This note,” she said.

She read it once in silence before giving him the English.

“‘The reader was first addressed concerning a memory he had told no one.’”

Cade looked at the year stamped near the top.

“Nineteen sixty-eight.”

“The card,” Miriam said. “There is no date beside the pencil.”

“Whose memory?”

“This is an index entry. We need the account.”

Marcus was watching him now.

Cade had not answered the question. He could not tell what his silence had supplied in its place.

He looked again at the short note, squeezed into a space somebody had left beside the description.

There was nothing there about a prison. Nothing about a blanket or five words spoken into it.

Cade drew his notebook closer.

“Who else has been allowed to copy this?”
