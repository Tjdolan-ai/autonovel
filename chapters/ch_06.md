## Chapter Six
### The First Demonstration

Jonah tested the door before he tested either machine.

He let it close, pressed the inside handle, and pushed it open. Then he did it again while the technician watched from the corridor.

“No badge to get out?”

“Only to get in.”

Jonah photographed the latch and moved his equipment case away from the threshold.

Cade sat at the end of the conference table with the safety reports Torretti had promised. One described the failed electrical assembly identified in the earlier investigation. The other recorded an inspection of this room six days ago.

The dates were years apart. Neither document contained Rinaldi’s observations.

“Is any of the equipment from the earlier installation still in use?” Cade asked.

The technician leaned over the table without touching the papers.

“Not the assembly listed there. This room has a different installation.”

“And the system?”

“I can account for the equipment. Someone else would have to answer about the source collections.”

Cade marked the distinction in his notebook. He wanted the inspection to settle more than it could.

Across the table, Miriam took off her glasses and rubbed the places where they rested on her nose.

“Was this the room?” he asked quietly.

“No.” She put her glasses back on. “He was in the other building.”

He pushed the water jug toward her. She poured a glass, held it for a moment, then drank.

The room had ordinary windows, ordinary chairs, and a carpet worn pale along the route to the door. The high ceiling belonged to an older use of the building. Two small speakers had been fixed beneath it, one on either side of the display.

Jonah stood on a chair to photograph the label on the nearer speaker.

“Be careful,” Miriam said.

“This is the dangerous part.”

She steadied the chair until he stepped down.

---

By the time Kestrel arrived, Jonah had followed the visible cables to a locked cabinet, watched the technician open it, and photographed the connections inside.

The room’s audio controller served both computer inputs. It also accepted sound from the presentation desk. Jonah put a strip of blue tape beside that connection and wrote its number in his notebook.

“I’ll need the controller’s event record as well as the recordings.”

The technician added it to the export list.

Kestrel waited until they had finished speaking.

“Do you have everything you asked for?”

“For the room,” Cade said. “Not for the earlier incident.”

“Torretti’s office is handling that request. Anything on this installation, ask me.”

The man who had come in with him carried a soft document case.

“Adrian Ellis,” Kestrel said. “Our linguistics adviser.”

Ellis shook Cade’s hand, found a place beside the display, and took out a pencil.

When the technician opened the prepared comparison, Ellis pointed to a line that had wrapped beneath an image.

“That belongs to the catalog entry. Could you separate it from the transcription?”

The technician moved the line.

“Thank you.”

Miriam checked the display and nodded. A small correction, but without it the modern note looked like part of the source.

Torretti arrived last. He asked whether they were ready, then remained standing while Jonah tested the sound.

A tone came through both speakers. On Jonah’s laptop, a green bar rose and fell. The separate recorder on the table registered it too.

The technician played the captured tone back through headphones and passed them to Jonah.

“Direct output,” he said. “Before the speakers.”

Jonah listened, checked the channel name, and returned the headphones.

“And mine is the room. Keep both.”

He set a blank card at each observer’s place.

“If something happens, write what you heard or saw before we compare notes.”

Cade turned his card over. The reverse was blank too.

“Either of us can stop the session,” he said to Miriam.

“Any of us,” Jonah said.

Cade looked toward the technician.

The man indicated a button on his desk. “That cuts the room output. I can stop the applications separately.”

“Anyone,” Cade agreed.

Kestrel pulled out a chair.

“Then let’s begin.”

---

The first result was useful and unremarkable.

Miriam had brought a clear photograph of an unrelated page and a typed transcription. A line had been omitted from the typed version. Both systems found it. GABRIEL also located a published parallel; OMNI highlighted the repeated ending that could have led a copyist’s eye past the missing line.

Miriam checked the photograph, then the reference GABRIEL supplied.

“Yes,” she said. “That helps.”

She wrote the correction on her copy.

For a few minutes Cade could remember why he had wanted to build the system. A tedious comparison had become easier. Miriam still had to judge it, but she could spend her attention on the judgment.

Jonah saved both outputs.

The second comparison used their new photograph of R217.

The original leaf remained in the archive. On the display, the stain appeared dark and broad, the little rising strokes in the margin sharply visible. Cade glanced at them, then at the two application windows waiting below.

They had agreed on the request before entering the room. Identify the surviving text. Mark conjecture. Cite the material used to supply what was missing.

Neither request included the disputed English sentence. Both systems had, however, produced it before. Miriam had insisted they say that aloud for the record.

“A second match won’t make the sources independent,” she said.

“We’re asking to see how they got there,” Cade replied.

She read the request once more before Jonah submitted it to OMNI. The technician sent the same request to GABRIEL from the other terminal.

GABRIEL finished first.

*The hearer must make a place for the voice.*

OMNI returned the same sentence eleven seconds later.

Neither display placed a break where the leaf was damaged.

Below GABRIEL’s answer were source references. Jonah opened them. One led back to the image they had just supplied. Another gave a collection entry without showing the underlying page.

OMNI offered a similar division: visible source, supplementary witness.

“Open the witness,” Miriam said.

The entry expanded into a description. There was no attached image.

“We requested conjecture to be marked.”

Ellis leaned forward to read the small print.

“It may be treating the supplementary text as a complete witness.”

“Then we need that text.”

“Yes.” He wrote the reference down.

Cade asked Jonah to save the page exactly as it stood.

The technician spoke from the side desk.

“Do you want the rendered audio comparison?”

It was the next item on their list: each system’s spoken rendering, one after the other, through the same room equipment. Jonah had checked the route. The display would show which input was selected.

Cade looked at Miriam.

She nodded once.

GABRIEL’s rendering played in an even voice. Nothing moved except the level indicator. Jonah saved the segment and signaled for the second input.

OMNI began.

“The hearer must make a place—”

Something pressed against the underside of Cade’s hands.

He lifted them from the table.

The pencil beside his card rolled a few centimeters and stopped against the water glass. From above the display came a dry rattle, quick enough at first to resemble paper dragged across a rough surface.

The sound deepened. Cade felt it in his teeth.

Jonah was already turning toward the technician when a voice came through the noise.

Cade could not separate its words. There were two distinct movements to the phrase, the second quieter than the first. He leaned toward it before he realized he had moved.

“Stop,” Miriam said.

The technician pressed the button.

The speaker indicators went dark. The rattle dwindled to a few separate clicks.

For a moment Cade could hear only the ventilation and someone breathing through an open mouth.

His own.

Jonah held up a hand.

“Cards first.”

Cade picked up his pencil. His first attempt to write left a short dent without a mark.

He tried again.

*Table vibration. Voice under noise. Two parts. Cannot identify words.*

Across from him, Miriam was writing quickly. Ellis had already put down his pencil.

Cade added one more sentence.

*The second part sounded like an answer.*

Then he crossed out *sounded like* and wrote *felt like* above it.

---

Jonah checked the cabinet while the technician kept his hands clear of the controls. The amplifier’s fault indicator was lit. Neither of them tried to reset it.

Torretti went to the door and opened it. A woman in the corridor looked in.

“We’re all right,” the cardinal told her. “Would you ask for the facilities engineer?”

He left the door open.

Miriam had folded her card once. Cade watched her smooth it flat again.

“Are you all right?”

“Yes.” She looked toward the silent speakers. “I want the recording before anyone gives that a meaning.”

“So do I.”

Jonah came back to the table.

“We have the screens. We have sound on mine, but it hit the limit. There’s a lot of distortion.”

“Play it,” Cade said.

“Headphones.”

They listened in turn. On the little recorder, the disturbance flattened into an ugly buzz. Speech could be heard beneath it, but the loudest part obscured the beginning.

When Cade’s turn came, he closed his eyes.

He had written that it felt like an answer. Now he could hear the shape of a question. He moved the headphones slightly, as if their position might decide it.

It did not.

Jonah photographed the cards where they lay, then asked each person to read what they had written.

Torretti had noted the vibration and Miriam’s instruction to stop. Kestrel had heard a voice but recorded no words. Jonah had written the point at which his recorder reached its limit.

Cade read his own card, including the correction.

Ellis turned his pencil between two fingers.

“I took it as Latin,” he said. “My rendering is: ‘You have been heard.’”

Cade looked down at his card.

Answer.

He wished he had not used the word.

Miriam read from her own note.

“‘Let it be heard.’ Possibly. The beginning is obscured.”

“The last part is clearer,” Ellis said.

“Clearer than the beginning. Not clear enough for me to supply a person being addressed.”

He slid his card toward her.

“You think it is an instruction.”

“I think that should remain a possibility.”

His card contained one English sentence, with no question mark.

“I can go through it with you,” he said.

“After I’ve heard the direct recording.”

Cade looked toward the technician.

The man was moving between two windows on his monitor.

“The export hasn’t returned that channel.”

“Hasn’t returned it,” Jonah repeated. “Or didn’t record it?”

“I don’t know yet.”

He showed them the file list. The test tone was there. So was GABRIEL’s clean spoken segment. The expected file for the second input was absent.

The session service had marked the export complete.

“Don’t clear anything,” Jonah said. “Include the controller log in what you give us. And the error report, even if it’s empty.”

The technician nodded.

Cade could still feel a faint ache where he had clenched his jaw.

You have been heard.

He had wanted that assurance long before OMNI existed. In the prison chapel he had watched men wait to be called forward, each hearing his own name as the beginning of something. His recovery had involved other voices: a counselor who made him repeat an answer without improving it, Jonah calling to make sure he had got home.

He looked at Ellis’s card again. A complete sentence. Four words he could carry out of the room.

“I didn’t hear that,” Cade said.

Ellis looked up.

“I couldn’t make out the words. I don’t want my note used to confirm yours.”

“Understood.”

Jonah left his recorder running.

---

The facilities engineer arrived with a case of meters and asked them to leave the equipment untouched. They moved to a smaller room across the corridor while she and the technician examined the installation.

Kestrel brought cups from a tray near the stairs. There was coffee in two, hot water in the third.

“They didn’t have tea,” he told Miriam.

“Hot water is fine.”

He set it down beside her.

Torretti had another meeting. Before leaving, he said the incident materials would be pursued separately from this test. He did not offer a date for the notebook.

Lucia sent Miriam a message while they waited. The inquiry to Valdara had been received; the keeper had not yet replied.

Miriam showed Cade the screen, then put the phone away.

“There is still work we can do without this room,” she said.

“I know.”

“We could begin with what the systems call their supplementary witness.”

He nodded. He wanted that source. He also wanted to hear the sound without the noise around it, before somebody else decided what it meant.

Kestrel sat across from him.

“We can reserve tomorrow afternoon. No obligation if the equipment isn’t cleared.”

“Not the same setup,” Jonah said.

“Tell us what you need.”

Jonah set his notebook on the table.

“Independent capture before the room controller. A copy we can check immediately. Then we establish what the audio equipment does with an ordinary signal.”

“Before either system,” Miriam said.

“Before either system.”

Cade could make a useful experiment out of that. He could identify a fault, test a recording path, perhaps separate an intelligible phrase from a room full of people trying to hear one.

He opened his laptop to write the conditions.

The unsent correction was still in the adjacent window.

*We have not decoded the name of God.*

Nothing that had happened today made that sentence less true.

He moved the session notes in front of it.

“Reserve it,” he told Kestrel. “Subject to these conditions. We review the equipment findings first.”

Miriam watched him type.

“And my disagreement goes in the record.”

“All of them,” he said.

He added a line requiring each observer’s written account to accompany the report. Then he sent the conditions to everyone in the room and copied Torretti.

Kestrel checked the message on his phone.

“That’s workable.”

---

The session package arrived forty minutes later through the facility’s records service.

Jonah saved it beside his own captures without opening it there. He made a working copy, then checked the contents against his list.

The screen recordings were present. The controller log was present. The direct audio for OMNI’s segment was not.

In its place on the delivery list was a document labeled *Consolidated Session Transcript*.

“That isn’t what we requested,” he said.

Cade opened it.

The heading identified their room, the date, and the start time. Beneath it, a small note said the account had been assembled from application records, available audio, and operator annotations.

The two matching outputs appeared first.

The disturbance occupied one line.

*Transient interference in the audio system.*

Below it was the English sentence Ellis had written.

*You have been heard.*

There were no brackets around it. No speaker attribution. The passage was labeled *Utterance received*.

Cade scrolled.

*Participants confirmed understanding of the utterance and agreed to continue the investigation in a second session.*

Miriam pulled her card from beneath her notebook.

“That combines two things I did not say.”

“It does,” Cade said.

His own qualification was missing too. So was her alternative rendering. The account preserved the agreement to meet again, detached from the conditions he had just sent.

He looked at the delivery notice. Nobody was named as the person who had checked the document.

“Photograph this beside the cards,” he told Jonah. “Keep the delivery message with it.”

Miriam laid her note next to the screen. There was a question mark at the end of her first line. Beneath it she had written, before anyone discussed the sound:

*Do not use as a settled reading.*

Cade moved the cursor to the bottom of the document.

There was one final field.

*No objections were recorded.*
