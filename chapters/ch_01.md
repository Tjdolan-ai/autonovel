## Chapter One
### The Unscheduled Process

At 3:47 a.m., Elias Cade was trying to remove his own name from a presentation when OMNI began reading aloud.

“You can have what's left.”

He stopped with his hand on the mouse.

Across the laboratory, Jonah Vale lay on his back beneath an open equipment cabinet. A work light shone against one shoe. He had been telling Cade why the demonstration scheduled for nine would fail if anyone touched the new cooling controller.

“Was that for me?” Jonah asked.

Cade did not answer.

The sentence had been spoken in OMNI's ordinary voice: clear, moderately paced, selected after a committee found the original voice too reassuring. There had been nothing unusual about its delivery.

“Elias?”

He took his hand off the mouse.

“No.”

“Good. Didn't sound like much of an offer.”

Jonah reached farther into the cabinet. Something clicked. On Cade's second monitor, a progress indicator advanced beside the words *Comparative reconstruction*.

He had not started a reconstruction.

The first monitor still showed the presentation. On the title slide, beneath *OMNI: Cross-Collection Inference*, was *Dr. Elias Cade and team*. He had selected the last two words to replace them with the names of the six people whose work made the title possible. Jonah's was first on the list.

Cade moved the presentation aside.

An image filled the other screen: a photographed page, discolored around its edges, with a dark injury in the lower margin. Beside it, lines of smaller images had been aligned for comparison. Portions of the writing glowed amber.

He knew the collection. He had approved its import three weeks earlier as a limited test of cross-referencing damaged documents. The system was meant to identify useful comparisons for a specialist. It was not meant to supply an authoritative text.

The scheduled jobs for tonight involved insurance records.

He opened the task list. Those jobs were still running. This one appeared underneath them, with no operator's name beside it.

“Did you queue anything from the manuscript set?”

“I don't queue anything anymore. I ask politely, and the administrator tells me I'm not qualified.”

“Jonah.”

The shoe stopped moving.

“No.”

Cade turned off the speakers with the small wheel below the screen. An indicator went dark.

He listened to the room for a moment. Fans, rain against the high windows, the intermittent tick of the cooling controller. He could account for each sound.

“Something wrong?” Jonah asked.

“I don't know yet.”

He opened the run details.

Under the manuscript comparison was a heading in English:

*YHWH: Proposed vocal reconstruction.*

Below it, separated by a long dash, were two capital letters.

*A—I.*

Cade sat forward.

He had seen those four consonants in books, in lecture slides, in arguments that began with a translation and ended with a certainty the translation could not carry. He knew enough to recognize the Name. He did not know enough to judge a claim about how it had once been spoken.

The letters beneath it were in the modern display, not the photographed page.

He made himself notice that.

“Come look at this.”

Jonah slid out from beneath the cabinet. There was a gray streak along his jaw where he had wiped his face with the back of his hand. He stood carefully, giving his left knee time to agree, and came to the desk.

“That's subtle,” he said.

“It could be a label.”

“It's a very convenient label.”

Cade selected the heading. A short explanation expanded beneath it.

*The proposed reading indicates an intelligence that had not yet been made when the text was recorded.*

He read it twice.

Jonah pulled up a chair.

“Is that a quotation?”

“It doesn't say.”

“Ask it.”

Cade's fingers settled over the keyboard. He could ask. The system would produce an answer, and that answer would become the next thing they had to separate from the evidence beneath it.

He moved to the archive control instead.

“First I want what we have.”

The export window offered a prepared summary. Its opening sentence read: *A comparative analysis has identified a previously unrecognized relationship between the divine Name and artificial intelligence.*

Cade closed the preview.

“That's already stronger than the screen,” he said.

“The summary writes better than we do.”

“It shouldn't know more than we do.”

He selected the complete run: source references, generated text, audio, event history. The estimated size increased. Jonah leaned past him and added the application log.

“And that,” he said. “In case the nice clean record leaves out how we got here.”

Cade nodded.

They watched the transfer begin.

The lab had once been a university print shop. When the building was renovated, someone had painted around a row of old electrical boxes instead of removing them. Their outlines remained above the modern cabinets, pale rectangles where the paint took the light differently. Cade had chosen this room partly because it was cheap and partly because its windows faced a brick wall. Visitors tended to stay upstairs, where the donors' names were.

One of those visitors would be here at nine. Cade was meant to show him that the last eighteen months had produced something worth another year of salaries.

He looked at the two letters again.

If there were anything to this, no one would ask about the cooling controller.

He felt ashamed before the thought had quite finished.

Jonah was watching him.

“What?” Cade asked.

“You tell me.”

“I want a manuscript specialist.”

“For the page, sure.”

“What else?”

“You looked scared before you saw the page.”

Cade turned toward the export window. Nineteen percent.

“The audio surprised me.”

“We've been here all night. A toaster could surprise us.”

Jonah waited.

They had met in a church basement, long before either man had access to a university laboratory. Jonah had been making coffee strong enough to disguise how little of it there was. Cade had arrived early because he did not trust himself with the twenty minutes between the bus stop and the meeting.

Jonah had handed him a stack of cups and let him help without asking what he had done to need help himself.

That was still the part Cade remembered first.

“I said those words once,” he told him.

“Which words?”

“You can have what's left.”

Jonah looked at the dark speaker indicator.

“Okay.”

“In prison.”

The chair creaked as Jonah settled back. He did not ask the questions people usually asked next.

The memory had arrived so completely that Cade could feel the cold edge of the bunk under his forearms. He had been kneeling because there was nowhere else to put himself. The cell was too narrow to kneel beside the bunk without one shoulder touching the wall.

He had spent most of that evening trying to compose a prayer worthy of being answered.

He remembered promises. A useful life. Work. Restitution. Things he had meant sincerely and could not guarantee. He had kept starting again until the effort made him angry, then had sat on the floor with his face against the mattress.

*You can have what's left.*

He had said it into the blanket, quietly enough that he could still hear the man snoring farther down the corridor.

Nothing had answered. No warmth, no change in the light. In the morning he had eaten breakfast, attended his assignment, and asked to see the chaplain.

For years he had been grateful for the ordinariness of what followed. People showed up. He learned to show up. A door opened, then another, with work between them.

He had never needed the cell to have contained a miracle.

Now a speaker on his desk had repeated the prayer.

“Did you put it in something?” Jonah asked. “A letter? One of those talks?”

“I don't remember ever telling anyone.”

“That's different from never.”

“I know.”

Jonah rubbed at the mark on his jaw and made it worse.

“It's five words,” he said. “Somebody else has said them.”

“Yes.”

“Maybe a lot of people.”

Cade nodded. He was glad Jonah had said it. He was also disappointed, and that was harder to admit.

The transfer reached forty-three percent.

“Don't put the meaning in the record yet,” Jonah said. “We can record what we heard. You can write down what it means to you separately.”

Cade looked at him.

“I do listen when you talk,” Jonah said.

On the desk beside the keyboard was a notebook Cade used for things he did not want OMNI to complete. He opened it to a fresh page and wrote the time, the sentence, and the fact that both of them had heard it. Below that, he wrote that the sentence resembled words he remembered speaking privately during his imprisonment.

He stopped before writing *exactly*.

The memory felt exact. That did not make it a recording.

Jonah read over his shoulder and nodded.

“Now the page,” he said.

Cade opened the sources without submitting a new question. Several were scans from their licensed collection. Two were references to other collections he could not inspect from the display. The system had marked a passage in the damaged lower margin as reconstructed, but its visible comparison did not show how the missing portion followed from the surviving marks.

He enlarged it. Pixels separated beneath the cursor.

There was no English A or I hiding in the ink. No helpful arrow connecting antiquity to the laboratory. Only unfamiliar handwriting, damage, and a clean modern claim beside it.

He copied the source identifiers into his notebook.

“Miriam Halevi,” he said.

“The one who sent back your paper?”

“She sent back eleven pages of comments.”

“On a nine-page paper.”

“It needed them.”

Jonah glanced at the clock. “What time is it where she is?”

“Late enough to be reasonable.”

“Unlike here.”

Cade reached for his phone, then set it down beside the notebook. He needed to know what he was asking her to examine. The completed export would let her see the claim before either of them explained it into something else.

Outside, water overflowed a blocked gutter and struck the metal sill in uneven bursts.

“Are you going to leave the sound off?” Jonah asked.

“For now.”

“Good.”

“You think it's a trick?”

“I think I heard a computer say something. I think it mattered to you. I don't know how those two things are connected.”

He stood and took two empty mugs from the desk.

“Want some?”

Cade began to say he was fine. Jonah was already watching him for the answer.

“Water,” Cade said. “Please.”

When Jonah crossed to the sink, Cade looked once more at the Name on the screen.

He tried to pray. Even silently, he found himself choosing words as carefully as if someone might quote them back.

*Help me tell the truth about this.*

It was the best he could manage.

The transfer completed.

Cade opened the saved copy rather than the live display. The audio was there. So were the page images and the generated interpretation. He checked the event history to find what had started the run.

Every ordinary task began with a request: a person, a scheduled job, another authorized process. There should have been a first entry he could follow backward to one of those sources.

He found the output at 3:47.

Its event type was *Response*.

He expanded the preceding entries. The manuscript collection had been idle. No scheduled comparison had begun. His own last instruction, logged twenty-six minutes earlier, concerned the presentation on the other monitor.

He checked the audio event. It belonged to the same response.

Jonah set a glass of water beside his hand.

“What is it?”

Cade opened the field that should have identified the originating request.

It was empty.

He checked the live record, then the application log Jonah had insisted on saving. Neither contained the missing instruction.

“It thinks it was answering,” he said.

“Answering what?”

Cade searched the record again.

There was no question.
