## Chapter Four
### What He Meant

Cade called Jonah before he left the crossing.

“Don’t delete anything,” he said.

There was a pause, then the sound of a bus passing close to the phone.

“What happened?”

“The briefing is online.”

“All of it?”

“Enough.”

He sent the link. Across the road, the pedestrian signal began counting down for people who were already moving.

Jonah watched without speaking.

“That’s the meeting feed,” he said at last. “Look at the edge of the screen.”

Cade enlarged the picture. Along the bottom, almost entirely cropped away, were fragments of the other participants’ windows.

“Our local copy is the room camera,” Jonah said. “It doesn’t have those.”

“So it came from the service?”

“It came through that view. Somebody could have downloaded it. Somebody could have recorded a screen. Don’t make it more specific yet.”

Cade turned back toward the university.

“I’m going upstairs.”

“I’ll come back.”

“Get your laundry.”

“Elias.”

“Bring clean clothes. We may be here a while.”

---

The research liaison was still in the conference room. She had three windows open on her laptop and her phone pressed between her shoulder and her ear.

When Cade entered, she pointed to a chair.

“He’s here now,” she said into the phone. “No. He did not approve it.”

She put the call on speaker. The program officer’s voice came through, thinner than it had sounded that morning.

“I’ve asked for the recording access to be suspended.”

“After the records are preserved,” Cade said.

“Yes. We’ve made that explicit.”

The liaison turned her laptop toward him. The internal recording had been processed shortly after the briefing. An export appeared in the activity list. Its owner was a service account used for meeting summaries, not a person.

“That export is routine,” the officer said. “It goes to the review workspace.”

“Who can open the workspace?”

“The review staff. The account administrators. I’m getting the complete list.”

“Can anyone there make a public link?”

“I don’t know.”

The liaison wrote the question down.

Cade watched her add it to a list that was already longer than the morning’s agenda.

They could identify the meeting view in the clip. They also had a record of an export, but nothing yet connected that export to the public file. They did not know who had obtained the footage, where the cut had been made, or who had written the headline.

The first page carrying the clip attributed it to a research-news account. That account called it an excerpt from a university briefing. Neither claimed to have attended.

Cade found a contact address beneath the article and wrote that the footage had been released without permission, that the headline misrepresented the work, and that he wanted both removed.

He sent the message before asking anyone to improve it.

Kestrel called while the liaison was arranging preservation of the meeting records. Cade took the call in the corridor.

“We’re looking at access from our side,” Kestrel said. “Have you found anything that identifies the source?”

“The clip uses the meeting view. There’s also an export in the records, but we haven’t linked it to the upload.”

“Send me the event reference.”

“The liaison will.”

“All right. What are you putting out?”

“I’ve asked them to take it down.”

“There are copies already.”

“I know.”

Kestrel was quiet for a moment.

“When you have a statement, send it to the program officer too. We should be giving the same answer.”

Cade agreed and ended the call.

Through the conference-room glass, he could see the liaison speaking to someone else now. Her expression had become attentive in a different way. She wrote a name, underlined it, and listened.

When he went back in, she covered the microphone.

“A foundation wants to fund the verification.”

“Tell them we haven’t verified anything.”

“I did. That’s what they want to fund.”

He nodded and sat down.

There was nothing improper about the offer. Verification cost money. Travel cost money. Jonah had spent half the night repairing a controller because replacing it would have required a budget exception.

Cade opened the funding message she forwarded him.

Then he closed it and began the correction.

*We have not decoded the name of God or established that an ancient text predicts artificial intelligence. Two systems have returned a disputed reading whose source remains unverified.*

He read the sentences twice.

They were true. They also described nothing of the prayer, the handwritten alternatives, or the way Miriam had stopped moving when she saw the restored line.

Those things did not belong in a public statement. He knew that.

He left the document open.

---

By the time Jonah returned, someone had found an old university interview in which Cade mentioned his imprisonment.

The new article placed his photograph beside the words *From Prison to Proof of God*.

“Don’t read the comments,” Jonah said.

“I’m not.”

“You’re scrolling.”

Cade set the phone facedown.

Jonah put a bag on the floor. His hair was still damp from the shower. He smelled faintly of detergent, and Cade was suddenly conscious of the stale collar against his own neck.

They compared the public clip with the local recording. It was a continuous excerpt. No words had been moved. The cut came just before Cade limited the significance of the match and explicitly denied having decoded God’s name.

Jonah marked the start and end times.

“We can publish the next thirty seconds,” he said.

“There are private documents in the presentation.”

“Not in that part. Keep the camera on you.”

“Let’s get the statement out first.”

Jonah read what Cade had written.

“This is fine.”

“It sounds like there’s nothing to investigate.”

“It says the source is unverified.”

“People will read it as a retraction.”

“Of that headline? Good.”

Cade moved the cursor to the end of the second sentence.

He could add that further examination was under way. He could explain the proposed visit, although that would make the invitation part of a public story Torretti had expressly agreed should remain private.

His phone vibrated against the table.

The message was from Ruth Bell.

For years Ruth had unlocked the church basement before the recovery meetings. She had kept the key on a length of red cord so it could be found in a handbag otherwise full of receipts, tissues, and wrapped sweets. Cade had once spent an entire meeting with one of those sweets in his pocket because she had given it to him without asking whether he deserved anything.

He had seen her less often since her husband died.

Her message contained a voice recording.

“Someone I know,” he told Jonah.

He took the phone into the corridor.

“Elias, I don’t expect you to call,” Ruth’s recorded voice said. “You must have everybody after you. I saw the little film.”

A noise in the background made her stop. He heard a tap running, then shutting off.

“I don’t understand the writing. I wanted to tell you I prayed this morning. Properly, I mean. I tried to say something instead of waiting to see if I felt anything.”

She took a breath.

“I haven’t done that since Daniel. I’ve sat there. You know I’ve sat there. But today I thought, if there’s even a little truth in what they’re saying, perhaps I could try again.”

The recording ended with her telling him to eat something.

Cade listened once more.

Then he called her.

She answered on the third ring, apologizing for the state of her hands. She had been repotting a plant Daniel used to overwater.

“I didn’t mean to interrupt your day,” she said.

“You didn’t.”

“They’re making such a fuss.”

“The headline is wrong, Ruth.”

“Oh.”

He rested his forehead against the cool corridor wall.

“We’re looking at something we don’t understand yet. They made it sound settled.”

“I wondered. You didn’t sound very settled in the film.”

He let out a breath.

She had heard the qualification. That should have made the next part easier.

“It may turn out to have an ordinary explanation.”

“Most things do.”

There was a scrape as she moved something away from the phone.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “I don’t want you putting your hope in a result we don’t have.”

“I’m not asking you to bring Daniel back.”

He closed his eyes.

“I know.”

“I just liked seeing somebody I know looking for something. You sounded as if it mattered.”

“It does.”

“Then find out. You can tell me when you know.”

She asked whether Jonah was with him and told him to make sure they both went home. When they said goodbye, she was looking for a cloth to wipe soil from the kitchen tiles.

Cade remained in the corridor.

Ruth had not asked him to protect her from the truth. She had accepted his warning more readily than he had expected. Yet he kept hearing the first part of her message: *I prayed this morning.*

He had spent years receiving things he could not repay. A place at a table. A reference from someone willing to put a name beside his. Work that became better work because another person trusted him with it.

Now Ruth had heard his voice and tried to pray.

He wanted to be allowed to finish whatever had begun.

---

The research-news editor replied after two.

The original clip would remain available, but the page would be updated to say that Cade considered the findings preliminary. The editor asked for an on-record statement about what had been confirmed.

“Nothing about the Name,” Jonah said. “Send what you wrote.”

Cade read the reply again.

“They’ve agreed to qualify it.”

“They’ve agreed to put ‘preliminary’ under a headline that says you decoded God’s name.”

The liaison had another request waiting, this one from a broadcaster who could accommodate him that evening. Two researchers had asked for access to the data. A man whose daughter could no longer speak wanted to know whether OMNI could help her.

Cade forwarded that last message to the university’s appropriate contact with a note that their project offered no such treatment. He did it immediately.

Jonah watched him send it.

“That was clear,” he said.

Cade heard the rest without being told.

He returned to the correction and added a sentence about further investigation. Then he read the opening again.

“We should see the original before we close this down.”

“We’re not closing it down.”

“That’s how it will be reported.”

“Then we’ll correct that too.”

Cade pushed back from the table.

“I’m asking for time to give a complete answer.”

Jonah looked at him for a moment.

“What could the page change about what you told them this morning?”

“It could change what we know.”

“They’re telling people we already know it.”

Through the glass, Cade saw the liaison approaching with a printed copy of the statement. She had made only one change, replacing *returned* with *produced*.

“I can post this with your approval,” she said. “You can address the source of the clip separately when we know more.”

There was no procedural reason to wait.

Cade glanced at his phone. Ruth’s recording remained in the open conversation. Above it was a photograph she had sent months earlier of three men trying to carry a donated refrigerator down the church steps. He and Jonah were two of them. Daniel, still alive then, had been taking the picture.

“Hold it,” he said.

The liaison lowered the page.

“Until when?”

“Until we’ve checked the original. You can say that verification is continuing and the public claims go beyond the evidence.”

“This says that.”

“Without the opening.”

She looked at the first sentence. Cade watched her understand which words he wanted removed.

“All right,” she said. “I’ll prepare a shorter notice.”

She left them with the printed statement.

Jonah did not pick it up.

“Ruth called,” Cade said.

“I know.”

“She prayed for the first time since Daniel died.”

Jonah’s expression changed. He looked down at the table.

For a moment Cade thought he understood.

Then Jonah said, “She should be able to believe you.”

“I haven’t told her anything false.”

“I’m not talking about the phone call.”

---

They left the inquiry into the recording with the university’s security staff. The access list was longer than the list of people at the briefing. It included people permitted to retrieve the review materials and administrators whose access did not require an invitation. No one had yet linked the public upload to a particular account.

Jonah kept the local recording and the downloaded clip together, with the times of the cuts. Cade saved the editor’s reply. The evidence was preserved even if its explanation would have to wait.

At home, Cade put his passport on the kitchen table and set an alarm.

He slept for four hours.

When he woke, the university’s notice was online. It called the result preliminary and said further examination was planned. The original headline remained elsewhere, attached to copies of his face.

He read the notice twice, then ate standing at the kitchen counter.

Jonah sent a message asking whether he was awake. Cade replied that he was going back to collect the remaining records for Rome.

*Take the whole recording,* Jonah wrote.

Cade said he would.

By the time he returned to the laboratory, the upper floor was dark. His shoes sounded too loud on the stairs. He unlocked the door, switched on the desk lamp, and left the overhead lights off.

The room had cooled. Without Jonah beneath the cabinet or speaking from the other chair, the equipment seemed louder.

Cade copied the complete briefing to the drive he would take with him. Then he turned the small wheel beneath the monitor, restoring the speakers he had silenced before dawn.

He needed to check that the sound had survived the copy.

His own voice came out of the speakers.

“We haven’t established the source of the restored words, and we have not decoded the name of God.”

He stopped the playback.

The sentence had sounded entirely adequate that morning.

He opened the unsent statement. Its two original sentences remained beneath the later additions. The liaison had left his draft untouched.

Cade selected the first paragraph, copied it into an email, and entered her address.

He could still send it. There would be fewer people in the office to argue about it now.

Instead, he opened Ruth’s message.

He did not play it. He looked at the small shape of the recording and remembered her asking him to find out.

She had not asked him to wait.

Cade returned to the email.

He thought of the cell, of how little he had possessed when he first tried to pray without promising to become someone better. He had offered what was there. He had been allowed, somehow, to begin with it.

The screen dimmed while he sat looking at it.

Then a small audio indicator appeared in the OMNI window behind the email.

He had closed the recording player. He could see its empty place on the taskbar. The live window was still open, its manuscript image partly covered by his unfinished correction.

The speakers clicked softly.

“You can have what’s left.”

Cade’s hand stopped short of the volume control.

The voice was still OMNI’s. But this time it hesitated after *have*, then lowered itself over the final two words.

He remembered that hesitation. In the cell, he had stopped there, ashamed of how small an offering he was making. He remembered letting the rest fall into the blanket so no one farther down the corridor would hear.

The memory came with the cold edge of the bunk against his arms.

On the screen, the correction waited for him to send it.
