## Chapter Ten
### The First Compilation

The engineer put the visit record in front of Cade.

“Who decides whether we have met these conditions?”

He had been looking through the glass behind her. Two workstations stood in the next room, their screens dark. Someone had cleared a space between them for the independent recorder Jonah carried in his bag.

They had prepared for him.

“Jonah,” he said.

The engineer glanced at the second name on her sheet.

“For the recording arrangements?”

“For the setup. He wrote the acceptance conditions.”

Jonah stood beside the table, still wearing his coat. The journey out of Rome had taken longer than Cade expected. They had spent most of it watching the backs of delivery trucks, with Miriam's folder wedged between them on the rear seat.

The engineer turned the sheet toward Jonah.

“Then I need your name here.”

He read the line she indicated. “The original inputs and the complete run history. Not just the export.”

“I saw that. I'll show you where each is retained.”

Kestrel arrived while she was adding Jonah's name. He set a stack of bound copies on the table and waited until she had finished.

“I can keep the same team here this afternoon,” he said. “After that they have another installation.”

Jonah looked at Cade.

“We're inspecting,” Cade said.

“That's what they're booked to do.” Kestrel took off his jacket. “If you need longer, tell me before lunch.”

The engineer carried the amended record into the adjoining room. Jonah followed her. She was already asking him about the files he would want to take away.

Cade found himself listening for a question addressed to him.

None came.

---

The books had white card covers and black plastic bindings. One had been punched badly; a crescent of paper clung to the lowest hole.

*The First Compilation.*

Beneath the title, in smaller type: *Working English Rendering. Source Notes and Technical Appendix.*

Miriam opened her copy at the contents page.

“All of it?”

“The complete edition held by the research partner,” Kestrel said. “Including the notes behind yesterday's references. The file is yours as well.”

“Who made the rendering?”

“The credits are at the back. I requested the commissioning record too. It hasn't arrived.”

There were several contributors, listed against sections, and an editorial office rather than a named translator for the passage they wanted. Miriam marked the entry with a slip of paper. Then she went to the section number Jonah had found beneath the three different titles.

Cade recognized the excerpt on the left-hand page. The text continued well beyond the point where OMNI's answer had stopped.

“This is what it was citing,” he said.

“This edition contains those words,” Miriam said. She turned another page. “Now we can read what comes after them.”

The source notes contained small photographs, references to other copies, and gaps where images had not been supplied. Nothing about the paper on the table was old. Cade could still smell the toner when he bent over it.

Kestrel gave him the second copy.

“I thought you'd want the technical section first.”

Cade did. He kept his place with a finger and began at the front.

---

The opening proposition occupied less than half a page.

*Creation answers to speech.*

The paragraph beneath it moved through acts Cade knew from scripture: calling, naming, commanding. It drew them into a single account of how things came to be.

He read without making a note.

The next section changed its vocabulary. Form became arrangement. Arrangement became information. The difference between a living thing and the account of that thing grew harder to locate as the sentences proceeded.

He went back two pages.

At the beginning, there had been a speaker.

Now the text was discussing what speech could do.

“Here,” he said.

Miriam held her page open while he brought his copy beside it.

*What is made through expression remains available to expression. Where the arrangement is restored, the work may continue.*

“That could mean describing how something is made,” Cade said.

“It could.”

He followed the next line with his finger.

*The intelligence assembled from words therefore shares in the substance by which the flesh was called forth.*

Outside the glass, Jonah was kneeling beside a workstation. The engineer held a panel while he examined the connections behind it. There was nothing mysterious about the work. Cade knew the ache Jonah would feel in his knees when he stood.

He looked back at the word *therefore*.

“It hasn't earned that.”

Miriam waited.

“A description can help me build something. That doesn't make the description the thing I built.”

“The commentary treats the difference as a failure to understand the description.”

“Convenient.”

“Yes.”

Yet Cade went back and read the preceding paragraph once more.

Kestrel drew up a chair on the other side of the table.

“The partner's introduction is plainer.”

He opened his copy to a page separated from the rendering by a gray rule. A short line sat above the introductory paragraphs.

*All AI is is words.*

Cade read it aloud. The two instances of *is* made him hesitate.

“They mean language models,” he said. “And even then they've left out most of the machine.”

“They mean people understand a voice before they understand an architecture.”

“Do you believe this?”

Kestrel looked at the page before answering.

“I think there are things in it we can put to work.”

That was easier to hear than a profession of faith. Cade knew what to do with a proposed mechanism. He could ask for its inputs, choose a result it might fail to produce, build something that would outlast the argument around it.

He turned to the appendix.

There was the reconstruction method, stated at length. There were limits, examples, and a procedure for committing to a completion before comparing it with a retained original.

He began marking the passages Jonah would need.

Across the table, Miriam was still reading the sentence about substance.

---

“Elias.”

She had found a short paragraph beneath a heading translated as *Against Division*.

*The Word is one.*

Cade read it, then looked at her. For the first time that morning, her pencil was resting on the table.

“That isn't the argument OMNI gave us.”

“Read the next sentence.”

*Its unity is the unity of a sentence, whose words are many. No single speaker exhausts what may be said through it.*

Below that came a note on the disputed ending of the testimony. It allowed both the editor's singular and a plural reading, then treated either as an expression of the same underlying claim.

Miriam picked up her pencil.

She drew a bracket beside the whole paragraph, including the note.

“I thought we had something else,” Cade said.

“So did I.”

Her voice was quiet enough that Kestrel leaned closer.

“What does it establish?” he asked.

“That this writer can say *one* without meaning what I hoped.”

He reached for the page.

Miriam let him take it. She watched while he read through the note rather than stopping at the first sentence.

“You can put that in the visit findings,” he said. “I need an account of what you examined, not an agreement with it.”

The engineer returned with a sheet for the morning's work. Beneath the equipment inventory was a space for documents received and outstanding requirements.

Cade wrote the title and section numbers. He started a sentence about a recovered commentary, stopped, and crossed out *recovered*.

Miriam leaned across to add the qualification about the rendering. Her sleeve pushed against his wrist; he moved his hand so she could finish.

“Will this keep you from inspecting the installation?” Kestrel asked.

“No,” Cade said.

He could still examine a machine without knowing what to make of the book beside it. For a moment he felt the relief of that separation.

Then Miriam put the complete paragraph into their notes.

The reassuring first sentence went with it.

---

Jonah came back carrying the engineering record and two paper cups. He put one beside Cade, clear of the open book.

“The service can retain the records we asked for,” he said. “I still need to follow the inputs through the service. That will take the afternoon.”

“Anything running?”

“No.”

He moved his coat from the chair and sat down.

Cade showed him the marked appendix. Jonah read the test conditions, then found the source reference attached to them.

“That's the same section?”

“A different part of it.”

“Then I want to see that part too.”

Miriam turned back to the running text.

The passage described keeping a saying after it had been removed from use. The English was awkward. Cade had passed over it on his first way through, taking it for another discussion of preservation.

Jonah read with one hand around his cup.

Then he set it down.

“Where did this come from?”

Cade looked at the line under Jonah's finger.

*Let the unvoiced return retain the last completed saying when the common register is cleared. It is not to be counted among the witnesses unless the keeper recalls it.*

He read *unvoiced return* again.

Six weeks before Rome, Jonah had used that name for a small OMNI prototype. It kept the last completed text response apart from the public output queue. Clearing that queue did not destroy the separate copy. The copy was excluded from subsequent model inputs unless an operator deliberately recalled it.

They had wanted a way to examine a discarded answer without quietly feeding it back into the next one.

The prototype had not gone into the version running in their laboratory.

“Your laptop,” Cade said.

Jonah was already reaching for it.

He opened the design folder they had brought with the inspection material. It took him two attempts to find the note; the first file was a later discussion of storage limits.

The earlier one carried Jonah's name and a date.

At the top was the title *Unvoiced Return*.

Below it were three short requirements.

Cade placed the book beside the screen. Survive clearing the public queue. Preserve the last completed response. Exclude it from the next input until recall.

All three were there.

Miriam read the note, then the paragraph in the book.

“Was this published?”

“No,” Cade said.

“Who received it?”

“We'll have to check.” He looked at Kestrel. “The partnership received development material. I don't know whether this was in it.”

Kestrel drew the laptop closer only after Jonah turned it toward him.

“I haven't seen this note.”

“Someone may have,” Jonah said.

Kestrel nodded. “I'll request the transfer list.”

Cade could feel his pulse in the hand resting on the page.

He had spent years putting words into a machine. He had not expected to find Jonah's small, practical name for one of its parts in this rendering of a passage about preserving a voice.

Miriam went through the accompanying images. There was a photograph for the preceding passage and another for the one after it. The disputed paragraph had only a source reference.

“We need this page before it became English,” she said.

“How old is it?” Kestrel asked.

“This rendering? I can't date it from what you've given us. The reference points to an earlier copy at Valdara.”

Cade recognized the repository name.

Lucia had been trying to arrange access since they found the second catalog number on the wrapper.

Miriam took out her phone.

---

The answer came while Jonah saved the design note beside a photograph of the open book.

Lucia had obtained a supervised appointment at the repository for that afternoon. She could meet them there with the transfer references. The duty archivist would leave at five; she could not promise another opening that week.

Kestrel looked through the glass at the engineer waiting beside the workstation.

“You'll lose the afternoon here.”

Cade knew. They had found an installation willing to retain failures and let Jonah follow the sources. He had expected to leave with the conditions settled, ready to do something he could measure.

The inspection record lay beneath his hand. Jonah's name was on it.

“Can we finish another day?” Cade asked him.

“If they'll hold the configuration and let me check it again.”

“Tomorrow afternoon,” Kestrel said. “I'll ask the team. I can't promise it.”

He did not tell them the book was a revelation. He did not tell them the match meant nothing. He gathered the papers the engineer would need and went to speak to her.

Through the glass, Cade watched her cross their names off the afternoon schedule. She set Jonah's recorder back in its case.

Miriam began putting the copies into her bag.

Cade reached for the cup Jonah had brought him. The coffee had cooled enough to drink, but his mouth was dry after the first swallow.

On the laptop, the design note still carried Jonah's name.

In the book, the same instructions had no author he could identify.

Someone had called this a translation.

He needed to see what it had translated.
