## Chapter Eight
### A Witness Before the Machine

“I heard her call the first time.”

Miriam read the sentence twice. The second time she moved the reading lamp, bringing the uneven line out of the paper’s shadow.

Cade had expected an account of a voice.

The man on the page was writing about his mother.

He had brought her broth and left it on the sill to cool. Later, when she called from the adjoining room, he had kept his eyes closed. He had been awake for much of the previous night. He wanted someone else to get up.

“‘In the morning,’” Miriam continued, “‘I told those who came that I had not heard her.’”

She stopped to examine a word.

“He says she died before morning. He does not say that answering would have saved her.”

Cade looked at the few lines separating the call from the death. The space seemed too small for the years a person could spend inside it.

The manuscript lay open in a low cradle. Four leaves, eight pages of writing, a wrapper with the reference T/14. The name at the top of the account had been scraped until the surface was rough.

Marcus sat opposite them with his case closed at his feet.

Lucia had secured the examination that morning. One account, its accompanying statement, its catalog history. She had persuaded Marcus to identify a document they could actually read; Torretti’s office had approved the release and a comparison of specified still images. Spoken playback and further experimental use remained under review.

There would be no demonstration in the conference room that afternoon.

Here, for once, they had the paper.

---

Before Miriam read farther, Lucia placed a bound volume beside the cradle.

“This answers part of your question about copies.”

The cover was dull brown, the spine repaired with cloth. On its title page, beneath the name of the collection, was the year 1932.

“A conservation bulletin,” she said. “They reproduced several items that needed work. This was one.”

She opened it at a photographic plate.

Cade recognized the shape of the damaged lower edge before he recognized the writing. The photograph showed the same leaves spread beneath a ruler. The scraped name was visible. So was a dark splash crossing the end of the mother’s account.

Jonah drew his chair closer.

“The whole thing?”

“Both faces of each leaf. The next plate has the remaining pages.”

He compared the page in the cradle with its reproduction. The stain cut through the same three lines. A letter above it leaned into the next word. Even the tiny hole beside the fold was there.

Lucia turned to the bulletin’s distribution list. Copies had gone to several libraries. One university had since digitized its volume; Jonah found that copy through the title and plate number and opened the image on his laptop.

For a moment there were three versions of the same damaged page in front of Cade: the leaf, the printed photograph, and the scan of another copy of the book.

Miriam followed the lines about the mother in the plate.

“They are here.”

“Before either system existed,” Cade said.

“Yes.”

She did not qualify it immediately. He was grateful for that.

“Does that date the account?”

“It gives us a date by which these words were in print. The manuscript is a copy. Its heading says it was taken from an earlier statement.”

“How much earlier?”

“The catalog assigns this hand to the seventeenth century. I want to examine that separately. We don’t need to settle it to establish what was published in 1932.”

Cade touched the edge of the bulletin, clear of the plate.

He had come prepared to ask whether somebody had written a warning after seeing OMNI’s output.

This warning had been available to readers before his parents were born.

It had also been available to copy.

Lucia brought out a reproduction register. An entry from 1996 recorded two photographic sets made for an external transcription project. One had been returned. The other had been retained under an authorization whose number appeared in the next column.

“The recipient’s details are in the authorization file,” she said. “I’ve requested it. The bulletin alone means we cannot give you a closed list of everyone who could have read the account.”

Jonah copied the authorization number. Then he saved the university scan with its catalog details.

---

The account resumed after a space of several lines.

The writer described sitting with two other men while a passage was read. He did not name the building. One of the men had questioned his reading; the other was there to record what followed.

“‘I heard my name,’” Miriam read, “‘but neither man had turned toward me.’”

Cade looked at the scraped place above the first paragraph.

“Then: ‘You were awake.’”

Miriam left a pause between that sentence and the next.

“‘I knew what was meant, though my mother was not named.’”

Marcus watched Cade across the table.

The words could have been spoken to almost anyone sitting with his eyes lowered. It was the writer who joined them to the room where his mother had died.

Cade knew how little distance there could be between hearing something and recognizing it.

“Does he say he told the others?” Jonah asked.

Miriam checked the following lines.

“Afterward. He says he confessed what had happened.”

“So their knowledge of the memory comes from him.”

“At least in this account.”

She made a note, then continued.

The man had difficulty reading aloud. When he lost a word, the effort of finding it made him lose the next. He dreaded the silence while other people waited. He had begun refusing duties that required him to speak.

The voice, he wrote, had offered him a way to answer.

“‘When you are called upon, the words will be there.’”

Miriam moved the pencil she used to follow the lines.

“He reports that he then spoke a passage without hesitation. He says he had not learned it.”

“What passage?”

“That is less clear. He gives the beginning, then refers to words supplied elsewhere.”

“Supplied by whom?” Cade asked.

“He doesn’t say.”

Marcus leaned forward.

“He was being offered something he wanted.”

“Something he needed,” Lucia said.

“That does not establish who offered it.”

“No. It doesn’t establish that helping him was wrong either.”

Cade looked at the line again.

There had been men in his recovery group who could repair an engine or remember every turn on a journey but could not get through the sentence they had come to say. Other people waited for them. Sometimes they tried to help and made it worse.

He understood the promise on the page. He disliked how quickly Marcus wanted to make understanding it a mistake.

“What happened next?” he asked.

Miriam turned the leaf.

“There is another statement.”

---

The second man’s account occupied less than a page.

He had heard the reader speak. He had not heard a separate voice address him. The passage the reader repeated was one they had practiced earlier, although he agreed the man had spoken it more easily than usual.

Miriam read the last sentence slowly.

“‘I cannot testify to what he says was spoken to him.’”

Jonah looked toward the short catalog summary Marcus had brought.

“Let me see that.”

Marcus passed it across.

The summary described an address heard by the reader and his companion, followed by an improvement in the reader’s speech.

Jonah placed it beside Miriam’s handwritten translation.

“This makes him a witness to the voice.”

“He is a witness to the occasion,” Marcus said.

“That isn’t what it says.”

Marcus took the summary back and read it again.

Miriam pointed to the opening of the second statement on the manuscript. Then she found it in the old photographic plate. The same line was there.

The disagreement had not appeared recently. It had survived alongside the first account.

“Who wrote the summary?” Cade asked.

“It is unsigned,” Lucia said. “It belongs to the later catalog file.”

“Is this the sort of thing Rinaldi received?”

Marcus held the paper a moment longer.

“I need to compare his request with what was sent.”

It was a narrower answer than Cade wanted. It was also a question they could put to a record.

“Do that.”

Marcus set the summary beside the other documents rather than returning it to his case.

Jonah adjusted his camera to include the translated statements and the summary in one frame.

Miriam moved her pencil out of the photograph.

“Both statements,” she said. “Keep them together.”

---

Cade asked for a fresh sheet.

Lucia passed him one without asking why.

He wrote the date of the first unscheduled run, then the date of the second utterance. Beside each he wrote the name of the file Jonah had preserved.

Marcus watched him turn the page so everyone could see it.

“It used words I had said while I was alone,” Cade said. “Years before I built OMNI. When I was in prison.”

Marcus did not move.

“The first time, I recognized the words. The second time, something about how they were spoken.”

He had described this to Miriam and Jonah. Saying it in front of the others made the memory feel less safely attached to him.

“Were the words recorded then?” Marcus asked.

“Not that I know.”

“What were they?”

Cade looked at the paper.

Miriam spoke before he answered.

“We can document the claim without asking him to repeat a private prayer.”

She had not said he should keep it secret. The choice remained where it had been.

“For now,” Cade said, “record what I’ve told you.”

Jonah added a line beneath the file names: *Personal context supplied by Cade. No contemporary recording available for comparison.*

Cade almost asked him to change *supplied* to *remembered*. He initialed the line as Jonah had written it.

Marcus glanced from that page to the older testimony.

“Now you understand why I asked.”

“I understand where the question can come from.”

Cade heard the resistance in his own answer. He let it stand.

“This stays with the recordings,” he told Jonah.

Jonah nodded and took a photograph.

---

The damaged passage came near the end of the first statement.

A strip of paper was missing along the lower edge. Several lines descended into it, leaving the tops of letters with their lower parts gone. The old photograph showed the same loss.

Miriam asked Lucia to raise the lamp.

“Here he returns to the promise about speaking,” she said. “But some of the connections are lost.”

The prose surrounding the damage described speech no longer failing at the mouth. Below it, among the surviving words, was flesh.

Cade looked toward the bulletin.

The editor had printed a transcription after the plates. Beside the damaged section was a supplied reading, followed by a short English rendering.

*The Word became flesh.*

Cade knew the sentence. He had heard it in places with polished pews and in a room where the chairs had to be stacked afterward.

For an instant its familiarity made the damaged line look more complete.

“Is that what is on the page?”

Miriam placed a finger beside the editor’s marks.

“That is his proposed completion. These brackets show what he supplied. The loss crosses more than one word, including endings we would need to test his reading.”

“Could he have restored it because he knew the same sentence we do?”

“Yes.”

She compared it with the leaf again.

“It is a possibility. I would leave it marked as one.”

There was a reference after the restoration. Cade expected another shelf number. Instead it named a commentary without identifying a surviving copy.

“Have we got that?”

Lucia checked the packet list.

“No. This is everything filed with the testimony.”

Miriam copied the reference exactly.

They had the old statement, the separate witness, and a printed reproduction that carried both back before OMNI. They still did not have the text the editor claimed to use for the missing words.

Marcus closed the bulletin halfway, leaving a finger at the plate.

“You see how quickly the gap becomes a sentence.”

Miriam held out her hand for the book.

“We can keep the gap visible.”

He gave it to her.

---

Lucia checked the approved image list before Jonah opened his laptop.

The permission covered the damaged ending and its surrounding lines. They would make a text comparison, preserve the request and response, and keep the private account out of the submission. The separate safety review and their challenge to the session transcript remained unresolved.

Jonah showed the group the crop he intended to use. It included the broken edge. He put a wider photograph beside it so the boundaries could be checked. The wider photograph would stay in their record; only the crop would go to OMNI.

The editor’s completion was not in either image.

“The same system may have encountered the bulletin,” Cade said.

“Yes,” Miriam replied.

“Then we record that. We don’t call a match independent.”

Marcus looked at the laptop.

“And if it gives you more than a match?”

“We ask where it came from.”

Cade could hear how inadequate the answer might become. He could also see the alternative Marcus wanted: the folder closed, the account reduced again to the summary on the table.

He asked Jonah to keep the camera running.

“We continue with everyone able to see what was asked and what came back.”

Miriam read the request on the screen. It asked OMNI to transcribe surviving marks, distinguish supplied text, and identify the sources behind any reconstruction.

She removed a phrase that suggested the ending was a quotation.

Jonah saved the final request. He confirmed that spoken output was disabled, then submitted the approved crop.

While they waited, Cade returned to the short account of the second witness.

*I cannot testify to what he says was spoken to him.*

After everything that had happened in the conference room, Cade found himself grateful for the sentence.

OMNI’s response began with the surviving text. Damaged spans remained marked. Beneath them appeared a source reference whose underlying document was not attached.

Jonah saved the response as it arrived.

Then the field labeled *Proposed reconstruction* filled.

*The WordS became flesh.*
