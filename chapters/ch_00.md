## Prologue
### The Second Voice

*Rome — years earlier*

Father Matteo Rinaldi had said stop twice.

On the screen beside his hand, the transcript read:

*RINALDI: Yes. Continue.*

He looked through the observation window. The technician on the other side was bent over a console, listening through one ear of his headset. Behind him stood a man Rinaldi had not met. Neither appeared alarmed.

“That's incorrect,” Rinaldi said.

The technician lifted one finger. A moment.

Rinaldi had spent forty-one years studying the small alterations by which one person acquired authority over another person's words. A doubtful mark made into a full letter. A question copied as a declaration. An explanation entering the margin and, a century later, becoming part of the text.

This had taken less than a second.

He leaned toward the microphone.

“I am withdrawing my authorization. Do you understand?”

His words appeared faithfully beneath the first entry.

That helped. He could feel how much it helped, which troubled him.

The technician touched his headset. “We heard you, Father. We're stopping the sequence.”

Under the glass of the examination table, the manuscript rested in its cradle. A brown stain crossed the lower margin and obscured the end of a sentence. Three hours earlier, Rinaldi had explained that no responsible edition could supply the missing words with certainty.

The new system had supplied them in eleven seconds.

GABRIEL, the technician had called it. The name appeared above the transcript in discreet gray letters.

Rinaldi had asked to see the comparison sources. There had been nine. Then, when he asked which source contained the proposed ending, there had been an explanation of how the sources agreed.

It was a good explanation. That was why he had asked again.

“Please leave the display as it is,” he said. “I need a record of the error.”

“Everything is recorded.”

Behind the technician, the other man checked his watch.

Rinaldi took his pencil from the groove in the table and pulled the paper worksheet toward him. He wrote the time from the wall clock, followed by the words he had actually spoken. His hand shook at the end of *stop*. He resented the weakness. It would be easy, later, to see an old man's unsteady writing and decide that the machine had understood him better than he understood himself.

“Father?”

He raised his head.

The voice had come through the speaker above the table. It was quieter than the technician's, without the compressed hiss that accompanied the headset.

“Yes?”

On the other side of the glass, the technician was speaking to the man behind him.

“There is still time to correct what you have said.”

Rinaldi waited for the sentence to appear on the screen.

Nothing appeared.

The room contained a camera, two microphones, a speaker, and more concealed equipment than he could identify. A second voice was not, in itself, a mystery. Someone might be in another office. A recorded passage might have entered the wrong channel.

He looked toward the camera.

“Who is speaking?”

The technician turned back. “I'm sorry?”

“Who else is on this circuit?”

“Just us.”

“I have heard another person.”

The technician glanced at his colleague. The man moved forward, close enough to the window that Rinaldi could see his mouth, but did not speak.

“Could you repeat what you heard?” the technician asked.

Rinaldi wrote the sentence instead.

As he finished, the display changed. His withdrawal remained in the main transcript. In a smaller panel below it, a summary had appeared.

*Examiner requests correction of transcription. Authorization remains active.*

He pointed at it.

“No. That is not what I requested.”

The technician read the panel, then reached across his desk. A blue light above the window went out.

“The test is paused. I'll come in.”

For the first time since the voice spoke, Rinaldi let himself look away from the glass.

He turned the worksheet over. At the top of the blank side, he wrote a name.

*Miriam—*

He had promised to telephone her when they finished. She would still be in the reading room, pretending that her own work had kept her late.

*If they send you a summary, ask for this sheet.*

He considered telling her what the restored sentence had said. But she would ask what he had seen in the manuscript, and he would have to admit that the clean, completed line on the screen had already begun to interfere with his memory of the stain.

He wrote instead:

*I said stop. They heard me. The record says something else.*

At the door, a latch clicked.

Rinaldi waited.

Through the glass, he could see the technician with a hand on the inner door control. The man behind him had picked up a telephone.

The latch clicked again. The door remained closed.

“It sometimes holds during shutdown,” the technician said. “Stay where you are.”

Rinaldi folded the worksheet once and slipped it beneath the cover of his notebook. He rested his palm over it.

“Miriam is waiting upstairs,” he said. “Please ask her to come down.”

The technician nodded. Rinaldi could not tell whether he had heard.

There was a brief sound from the speaker, like a microphone being moved across cloth.

Then the quieter voice said, “You may begin again.”

Rinaldi looked at the transcript.

Only his own last request was there.

---

At 6:20 the following morning, Sister Miriam Halevi was asked to identify his effects.

His notebook was in a transparent bag with his spectacles and a broken pencil. The bag was sealed. When she asked to open it, the official said that she would have to wait until the equipment investigation was complete.

On the form he gave her, the space for cause of death had been filled in.

The space for her signature had not.
