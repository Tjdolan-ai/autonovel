## Chapter Five
### The Missing Reader

The original was smaller than Cade expected.

On his monitor, the damaged passage had occupied most of the screen. Here it rested in a shallow cradle beneath a reading lamp, the whole leaf scarcely larger than the notebook he had carried from home.

He could have covered the stain with two fingers.

“Your sleeve,” the archivist said.

He drew his arm back from the edge of the support.

“Thank you.” She adjusted the lamp rather than the leaf. “You can come closer. Just keep the table clear.”

Her name was Lucia Serra. She had met them at the inner door, checked both their names against the invitation, and asked Jonah whether he needed power for his equipment. When he said he could work from the battery, she had shown him where the cable could go if he changed his mind.

Now she moved an empty chair nearer the table for him.

Miriam stood opposite Cade. She seemed smaller without the screen around her, and more difficult to read. He could see the tendons move in her hand when she steadied the cradle.

Beyond the closed window, someone was dragging a wheeled bin across the courtyard. Its rattle grew louder, then stopped. Rome had come to him so far in fragments: a driver’s sleeve, a strip of bright stone outside a doorway, the weight of his bag as he lifted it through another inspection point.

He had not slept enough on the flight to put the laboratory at a comfortable distance.

Two nights earlier, after the voice spoke again, he had saved the audio and the surrounding records to a separate folder. He had written his recollection of the hesitation before playing the file back.

Jonah had listened at the airport.

“I hear the pause,” he had said.

That was as far as either of them could take it. There was no recording from the prison to place beside it. Cade had described the memory without asking Jonah to certify it.

The file was in Jonah’s bag now, along with the complete briefing and the public clip.

The original correction remained unsent.

Miriam indicated the leaf.

“This is the leaf in your image.”

Cade compared it with the printout beside his notebook. The edge of the stain, the narrow split near one corner, the slight displacement of a line near the margin: all were where the photograph put them.

Without enlargement, the handwriting required effort. He had to lean forward, look away, then return to find the same mark again.

“Can you see more here?” he asked.

“In places.” Miriam shifted to give him room. “The photograph loses some of the surface. It also makes certain distinctions look cleaner than they are.”

She pointed with the blunt end of a pencil, keeping it above the page.

“The damage does not end at a neat boundary. You can see traces here. Then something that may be a stroke, or may belong to the stain.”

“Enough for the sentence?”

“No.”

He looked again. The complete English rendering had been easier to remember than any of the visible marks. He could hear it while examining the place where most of it was absent.

*The hearer must make a place for the voice.*

Lucia laid a folder beside the cradle.

“These are the existing examination notes. The detailed condition report is being brought up.”

“How old is the writing?” Cade asked.

“There are several hands,” Miriam said. “The text, the marginal additions, the notes on the wrapper. I wouldn’t give you one date for all of them.”

She showed him where one mark pressed into the space left by an earlier line.

He had imagined the original as something the machines could be measured against. Yet the original, too, required a person to say which features belonged together.

“What can you be sure of?”

Miriam looked up from the page.

“That it did not begin with your software.”

The answer gave him less than he wanted. It gave him something.

---

The outer wrapper lay open beside the folder. A recent label identified the item as R217. Under it, in a different hand, was V/45.

Miriam turned the wrapper far enough to expose the older inscription without disturbing its contents.

“Lucia, could we see the neighboring wrappers?”

“For the old numbers?”

“Yes.”

Lucia checked the request list on her clipboard.

“The next two items are already prepared. I’ll bring the covers first.”

She left through a staff door. It closed softly behind her.

Jonah opened the examination folder. Between a recent condition summary and a sheet of photographic measurements was a small consultation card.

He stopped on it.

“This refers to something that isn’t here.”

Cade moved beside him.

The card recorded a request to examine the lower margin under a different lamp. There was a date, a reader’s name, and a pencil instruction beneath it.

*Reader observations retained with incident file. See I/6.*

“What kind of incident?” Cade asked.

Miriam took her hand off the cradle.

He looked at the name again.

*Rinaldi, Matteo.*

There was a brief handwritten addition beside the request. A choice of word had been crossed out and replaced above the line. The compact, crowded handwriting looked familiar.

“Is this the man who sent you the notes?”

“Yes.”

“Can we ask him what he saw?”

Miriam did not look away.

“He died.”

The courtyard noise had begun again. Something struck a stone edge, followed by a short exchange between people he could not see.

“When?”

She gave him the year.

“Father Matteo Rinaldi,” she said. “He was my teacher.”

Cade looked from her to the card.

“Was this his last visit?”

“His last recorded consultation before access was restricted. It isn’t a record of every person who has handled the leaf.”

“And the incident?”

“He died at a research facility associated with the collection. The official finding was an equipment accident.”

Jonah had put both hands flat on the table, clear of the papers.

“What was he doing there?”

Miriam glanced at the staff door, then back at them.

“Reviewing material for an earlier GABRIEL program.”

Cade felt the long journey contract around that single word.

“The same system.”

“An earlier program,” she said. “I don’t know how much of its equipment or software remains.”

“But you knew he had been working on this.”

“I knew he had worked on these passages. I wanted to establish which records your system had used.”

Her answer was precise. He recognized the effort required to keep it that way.

“You let us decide to come without telling us.”

“I meant to discuss it before any live test.”

“We’ve already had something happen in our laboratory.”

She looked at Jonah. Then at Cade.

“Again?”

“The same sentence. With a different delivery.”

“When?”

“The night after we spoke.”

Miriam gripped the back of the empty chair.

Cade saw the question she could fairly ask him. He had intended to explain in person, where he could show her the saved file and avoid trying to describe the difference over a telephone. He had even thought of that as care.

It did not make him less angry with her.

“We preserved it,” Jonah said. “He recognized something in how it was spoken. I can hear the difference between the files. I can’t tell you where it came from.”

Miriam nodded, but her attention had returned to the consultation card.

“I should have told you about Matteo before you agreed to the visit,” she said.

Cade waited for the qualification.

She did not add one.

The staff door opened. Lucia came in carrying two folders, saw their faces, and set them down at the end of the table.

“What do you need?” she asked.

Jonah pointed to the reference.

“I/6.”

Lucia read it. Then she looked at Miriam.

“That isn’t kept with the collection.”

“Who can authorize it?” Cade asked.

“I can ask.”

“Please do.”

She took the card’s reference, not the card, and left them with the open folder.

---

Torretti came to the reading room with a thin file.

Cade knew before he put it down that it could not contain everything they had requested.

“The incident summary,” the cardinal said. “And the administrative record closing the earlier work.”

“The observations are filed with the incident,” Cade said. “That is what we need.”

“I understand.”

Torretti opened the file. The first page gave Rinaldi’s name, the date, and the official classification of the death. Beneath it were several paragraphs about an equipment failure. The names in one section had been withheld.

“Where is his own account?”

“Whatever he wrote during the session was retained with the original evidence.”

Miriam looked at the page without touching it.

“His notebook,” she said.

Torretti nodded.

“You’ve read it?” Cade asked her.

“No. The letters I showed you were sent before the test. His notebook was among the effects they showed me afterward. It was sealed.”

Cade pictured the handwritten sentence on his monitor, all its small hesitations surviving on the paper she had kept.

There was another set of pages, somewhere, written closer to whatever had happened.

“I want access to the complete file,” he said.

“I cannot release it under a manuscript consultation,” Torretti replied. “It includes statements from other people. Some were given on terms I am not free to discard.”

“A man died.”

“Yes.”

The cardinal let the word stand.

“You invited us to examine a system connected to that death.”

“I invited you to examine the records and make a controlled comparison. You may inspect the proposed room and equipment before deciding whether to proceed.”

“I also need the safety findings from the earlier incident.”

“I will arrange for those to be made available.”

“And his observations?”

Torretti rested his hand on the file cover.

“I will request a review of what can be disclosed. I am not promising you the complete witness file.”

For a moment, Cade wanted to close his notebook and leave. It would have been a clean gesture, easy to explain afterward.

He thought of the headline still traveling with his face attached to it. He had withheld a clear correction until he could see the original. Now he was looking at a record of a man who had seen it, and the questions were worse.

Leaving would not answer them.

He could not tell how much of that thought was responsibility and how much was his need for the journey to have been worth making.

“We keep copies of everything you have released,” he said.

“Yes.”

“And a record of what we requested.”

“Of course.”

Lucia, who had remained by the door, wrote the request on the consultation sheet.

“Whose office should I put down for the response?” she asked.

Torretti looked at her.

“Mine.”

She entered his name, then placed the sheet beside Cade’s notebook so he could read it before she took it away.

Torretti left the summary on the table.

It was still thin when he had gone.

---

For a while, no one spoke.

Jonah photographed the released pages. Miriam went to the window and stood where the light crossed the floor. Cade could see the courtyard reflected faintly in the glass beyond her shoulder.

“Was he frightened?” he asked.

She turned.

“Before he went in. Did he expect trouble?”

“He expected an argument. He usually did.”

Her mouth moved, almost a smile, then stopped.

“He asked me to wait upstairs. We were going to eat afterward.”

“You were here.”

“Yes.”

Cade looked down at the card that had sent Rinaldi’s observations somewhere else.

“I kept his letters,” Miriam said. “For a long time that felt like doing something.”

She came back to the table.

“Lucia, may we see the wrappers?”

Lucia brought the two folders closer. She laid their covers in order without putting anything on top of the original leaf.

Miriam aligned the three recent labels.

R216. R217. R218.

Beneath them, the older marks followed the same sequence.

V/44. V/45. V/46.

“They arrived together?” Cade asked.

“That’s what I want to check.”

Lucia brought over a bound accession register. A cloth marker was already in place near the center. She opened it carefully and followed a column with a narrow strip of card.

“R216,” she said. “Formerly V/44. Then R218, formerly V/46.”

Cade leaned forward.

The next line began a different group of items.

“Where’s the middle one?”

“It has a current catalog entry,” Lucia said. “But the transfer is not entered here.”

“Does that mean someone removed it?”

“It means I cannot account for the transfer from this register.”

She turned back two pages to a key of collection prefixes and placed the strip of card beneath the relevant line.

“V is Valdara. The old monastic repository outside the city. It continued to hold material after the community left.”

“Still does?” Jonah asked.

“Some. It isn’t open as a public reading room. There is a keeper.”

“Can we reach him?”

“I can make an inquiry.”

Miriam compared the wrapper to the entry once more.

“The transfer ledger there may explain the missing line here.”

Lucia nodded. “And which other papers traveled with it.”

Cade asked to photograph all three numbers, the two recorded transfers, and the prefix key. Lucia checked the pages and moved the lamp to keep its reflection off the camera view.

“Include the edges,” she told Jonah. “So nobody has to trust us about which label belongs to which cover.”

He took a wider photograph, then the details.

There was something reassuring about the work. A number could be followed to another number. A place had a keeper who might remember a box arriving. None of it promised an answer, but for the first time in days Cade could imagine asking a question without waiting for a machine to compose the reply.

He returned to the folio while Jonah checked the photographs.

Near the damaged passage, a small sign recurred in the margin. Three short strokes rose from left to right, joined at their bases by a curved line. The second instance was less complete than the first.

“What does that mark mean?” he asked.

Miriam bent to look.

“I don’t know yet. It may identify a passage, or a reader’s contribution. We need to compare its uses.”

“Could it be a name?”

“It could be an abbreviation. I wouldn’t expand it without another example.”

Jonah looked from the page to the image on his laptop.

“Wait.”

He opened the original export from the laboratory and worked down through the run details. They had examined the event history repeatedly, looking for the absent initiating request. Below those entries was a collapsed group of display fields.

He opened it.

In the space labeled *Process label*, the report displayed a small sign.

Three rising strokes. A curved line joining them.

“Was that there before?” Cade asked.

“This is the copy we saved that morning.”

Cade leaned closer to the screen.

“I didn’t see that field.”

“Neither did I. We were looking for the request.”

The request field was still empty.

Miriam drew the two forms on a fresh sheet, the manuscript’s first, the screen’s second. The shapes resembled each other closely. She left space beneath them rather than writing an identification.

“The photograph contains the mark,” Cade said. “OMNI could have taken it from there.”

“Does your software use marks from a source to name a job?” she asked.

“A process can inherit a label from material it is given. I don’t know why this one did.”

Lucia wrote the repository’s name and contact details beneath the paired shelf numbers.

“Keep this,” she said. “I’ll request the transfer records.”

Cade placed the sheet beside the incident summary.

On one page, a dead man’s observations were withheld.

On the other was an address.
