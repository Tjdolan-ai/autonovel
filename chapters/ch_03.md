## Chapter Three
### The Other Machine

The second machine chose the same words.

Cade read them on his phone in the stairwell, where he had gone to keep himself from watching the laboratory clock. He had promised Miriam an hour. With six minutes left, she sent a file and asked him to come back to his desk.

*The hearer must make a place for the voice.*

He stayed on the landing for a moment. A cleaner was working on the floor below, moving a mop bucket through a doorway. One wheel struck the frame repeatedly before she bent to lift it over the threshold.

“Need a hand?” he called.

She looked up. “Got it.”

The wheel came free. She disappeared through the door.

Cade went back to the laboratory.

Jonah had Miriam on the screen already. Beside her image were two documents: the request they had authorized and the answer returned by GABRIEL.

“I checked our packet,” Jonah said. “The image, the reference, the question. That’s all.”

Miriam nodded. “It is what I submitted.”

Cade sat down.

GABRIEL’s answer was less polished than OMNI’s. It occupied a plain table beneath an institutional heading, with a run number and the name of the operator who had accepted the request. It listed three possible witnesses to the damaged passage. A separate section offered a restoration.

The English rendering matched OMNI’s exactly.

“We asked for existing witnesses,” Cade said.

“Yes,” Miriam said. “The restoration came with the retrieval.”

“Was that automatic?”

“The operator says it is part of the normal report. I’ve asked him to preserve the whole session.”

Cade opened the first reference. A photographed fragment appeared, legible at one edge and progressively less so toward the other. The second reference led to a catalog description without an image. The third displayed a notice that the item could be consulted only by arrangement with the custodian.

He looked back at the completed sentence.

“Can you account for all of it from these?”

“Not from what we can see.”

“But some of it?”

“Enough to make the comparison reasonable. Not enough to accept the restoration.”

She moved something beneath her desk lamp. A sheet of paper entered the lower edge of her camera view.

“I found my copy of the notes.”

She had photographed the enclosure on both sides. Now she sent the images directly to Cade, separate from the comparison request.

He read the corrections first. *Listener* crossed out. *Hearer* above it. Farther along, a change from *prepare* to *make a place*.

“Those choices are unusual together,” Miriam said. “Either could have been translated differently.”

He turned to the second image.

The writer had asked her to leave his uncertainty visible.

Cade rested his hand on the desk beside the mouse.

“You didn’t send this to GABRIEL.”

“No.”

“And you hadn’t sent it to us.”

“You have the first image I have made of my copy.”

Jonah leaned forward. “Your copy.”

“Exactly,” she said. “I don’t know how many he made.”

Cade studied the handwriting. The corrections were crowded into the available spaces, the work of someone unwilling to waste a sheet merely because his first thought had not survived his second. He could almost see the writer reconsidering the sentence.

Both machines had reproduced the reconsideration.

Neither had reproduced the doubt.

“Could the systems have the same material?” he asked.

“They are operated separately,” Miriam said. “That is what I can establish this morning.”

“It isn’t the same answer.”

“No.”

Outside the high windows, the rain had stopped. Water still fell from the gutter, one drop at a time.

Cade enlarged the institutional reference attached to the third item.

“This is where you are?”

“It is a collection I can request access to.”

“Can you request it?”

“I already have.”

Her tone changed slightly.

“They would like to speak to you.”

---

Kestrel arrived at nine with the agenda Cade had approved two weeks earlier.

It listed model performance, infrastructure costs, and the demonstration for the next funding period. He placed it beside a cabinet Jonah had finally closed, then moved a spare chair to the desk without asking anyone to move it for him.

“You look as though you’ve started without me,” he said.

“We haven’t started anything on that sheet.”

“Then I’m on time.”

Cade knew him better through correspondence than through meetings. His name appeared at the bottom of the agreement that paid for much of the laboratory’s computing time. When a procurement dispute had threatened to idle the project, Kestrel had returned a call on a Sunday and resolved it by Monday afternoon.

He had also asked for a progress presentation before the next allocation was approved.

Cade had expected that presentation to decide whether he could keep the team together.

Now he was unsure what he wanted to tell him.

Kestrel looked from Cade to Jonah, then at the water glass and the unopened food container beside the keyboard.

“Should we reschedule?”

“There’s a result I need to explain first.”

“All right.”

“It’s preliminary.”

Kestrel took out a notebook. “Then we’ll treat it that way.”

Cade began with the manuscript comparison. He described the unexpected run, the two letters, and the unsupported claim attached to them. He did not describe the prayer. When he reached GABRIEL’s matching restoration, Jonah placed the two saved reports side by side.

Kestrel read them in silence.

“You withheld your answer from the other system?”

“Yes,” Jonah said.

“And it returned this?”

“Yes.”

“That seems worth pursuing.”

Cade felt an immediate, disproportionate gratitude.

He had prepared to defend the inquiry against someone who thought it a waste of funded time. Agreement made him aware of how much he had wanted permission.

“There are several ordinary ways it could happen,” he said.

“I’d like to know which one did.”

Kestrel looked at the clock.

“Our program officer is waiting upstairs. Shall I tell her we need another date?”

“The custodians in Rome have offered a call in twenty minutes.”

“Then we have twenty minutes to decide what you need.”

They moved to the small conference room above the laboratory. Daylight made the wall display difficult to read, and Jonah lowered the blind while Kestrel introduced the program officer, a woman Cade had previously seen only as a name on grant correspondence. The university’s research liaison joined remotely.

The officer asked to record the briefing for the internal review. She was taking over the account from a colleague and needed to distinguish a change in research direction from an additional expense.

“Internal only,” Cade said. “No release, no announcement, no claim that we’ve authenticated anything.”

She repeated the restriction for the recording before asking him to begin.

Jonah saved a local copy as well.

Cade stood beside the display. For the first time that morning, he could see the result from the place where an audience would see it: the old page, the new letters, the two matching sentences.

It made a persuasive picture.

He replaced it with a list of the things they had not established.

“We’re investigating a claim that artificial intelligence was anticipated in the divine Name,” he said. “Two systems have now returned the same disputed text. If those results are genuinely independent, we have a finding worth examining.”

The program officer looked up from her notes.

“But the match concerns one damaged passage,” he continued. “It doesn’t authenticate the larger claim. We haven’t established the source of the restored words, and we have not decoded the name of God.”

He showed the handwritten alternatives, then the images GABRIEL had cited. When the inaccessible reference appeared, the officer asked whether a specialist on site could inspect it for them.

“Possibly,” Cade said. “Our collaborator has requested access. The custodians want to discuss the source material and our comparison process.”

“Do they want the system?”

“I don’t know yet.”

The call from Rome arrived before anyone could suggest an answer.

---

Cardinal Alessandro Torretti appeared in a room that looked smaller than Cade’s conference room. There were no ceremonial furnishings in view. Someone had set a stack of folders too close to his elbow; he moved them before greeting the people on the call.

Miriam sat beside him.

Cade found her presence reassuring, then wondered whether the arrangement was meant to have that effect.

Torretti listened while Cade summarized the question. He asked Jonah which files had left the laboratory and asked Miriam whether the comparison request contained the proposed answer.

“It did not,” she said.

“And you have kept both requests?”

“Yes.”

The cardinal nodded.

“I cannot certify what our software has found, Dr. Cade. I can arrange for you to inspect what it claims to have read.”

“The reference is restricted.”

“The collection is restricted. We can authorize named readers.”

“Would that include the original behind our scan?”

“Yes. The responsible curator will need time to prepare it.”

Miriam spoke then.

“You should see the physical page. There are things this image cannot answer.”

Cade looked at the two reports still open on his laptop. He had spent the morning enlarging photographs of things on the other side of an ocean. The prospect of placing the original beside the reconstruction felt almost like relief.

“What would you need from us?”

“The records you have described,” Torretti said. “Your explanation of the process. If feasible, a closed comparison using both systems under agreed conditions.”

“No combined training,” Jonah said.

The cardinal glanced toward Miriam.

“No merging of the systems or their material,” she explained. “They need to remain distinguishable.”

“Of course.”

Cade watched Kestrel write something in his notebook.

Miriam leaned closer to the microphone.

“Come and look at it, Elias. We can argue about what it means when we have the page in front of us.”

He looked at Jonah.

“Both of us,” he said.

“I understood that,” Torretti replied.

“Jonah preserved the initial run. He needs access to the same records I do.”

“We can include that in the invitation.”

Beside Cade, Jonah had stopped taking notes.

The cardinal offered two possible dates. Both were close enough that Cade thought immediately of the unrenewed allocation, the passports he had not checked, the demonstration they still owed the university.

Kestrel waited until the invitation had been settled in principle before speaking.

“The partnership can cover the travel,” he said. “It already includes collaborative validation. We can also provide a secure workspace for the records. Nothing needs to be sent through a personal account.”

“Who would have access?” Jonah asked.

“We’ll agree on the list with you. Or you can use the university’s arrangement if you prefer. The expense is covered either way.”

There was no demand for exclusivity. No condition Cade could point to and say that it explained the offer.

He should have felt relieved by that too.

“I need the scope in writing,” he said. “A closed demonstration. Review of the original sources. No use of our participation as evidence that the claim is true.”

“You’ll have it,” Kestrel said.

Across the screen, Torretti nodded his agreement.

Miriam was watching Cade rather than either man.

“All right,” Cade said. “Send the invitation.”

---

The documents arrived before noon.

Jonah read the access conditions twice. Cade added a sentence about retaining the complete records from every comparison and sent it back. The revised invitation included both their names.

There was a place at the bottom for an academic title. Jonah left his blank.

“You can put laboratory operations,” Cade said.

“That isn’t a title.”

“It’s what you do.”

“It’s some of what I do.”

Cade looked at him.

Jonah finished the form and pushed the chair back.

“I need to go home before I agree to anything else. I’ve got laundry in the machine.”

“When did you put it in?”

“Yesterday. Before this became an international incident.”

“It isn’t an incident.”

“Good. Then my clothes are the emergency.”

He picked up his bag, checked that the copied records were inside, and paused at the door.

“You coming?”

“In a minute.”

Jonah waited.

Cade closed the laptop.

They walked out together.

Outside, a delivery van stood with its rear doors open. Someone was wheeling cartons of printer paper into the administration building. Students passed beneath umbrellas they had not bothered to close, although the rain was over.

Cade had slept badly before deadlines, hearings, job interviews. He knew the peculiar clarity that came after staying awake too long, and he knew how often he had mistaken it for insight.

At the steps, he stopped.

“Does this feel too easy to you?”

“What part?”

“The invitation. The money. All of it.”

Jonah adjusted the strap on his shoulder.

“You’ve been trying to get people to pay attention to this project for years.”

“Not to this.”

“Then keep telling them which part they’re paying attention to.”

They reached the path where they would separate.

“When things started working out for me,” Cade said, “I used to think an open door meant I was supposed to walk through it.”

“And now?”

Cade looked back at the building.

“I know I can open some of them myself.”

Jonah waited a moment, then nodded toward the bag carrying their saved records.

“We can go look at a page,” he said. “That’s what we agreed to.”

It sounded manageable when he said it.

They parted at the crossing. Cade watched until Jonah reached the other side, then took out his phone to order a car.

There were three new messages from the university’s research liaison.

The first said she had received the revised invitation.

The second asked whether he had approved a release.

The third contained a link.

Cade opened it.

His own face filled the screen. He stood beside the conference-room display, one hand resting on the table. The video began in the middle of the briefing.

“We’re investigating a claim that artificial intelligence was anticipated in the divine Name,” he heard himself say. “Two systems have now returned the same disputed text. If those results are genuinely independent, we have a finding worth examining.”

The clip ended.

Above it was a headline.

**AI DECODES THE NAME OF GOD: SECOND SYSTEM CONFIRMS DISCOVERY**

He played it again.

The qualification was there. *If those results are genuinely independent.* Whoever had cut the recording had left it intact.

What followed was gone: the damaged passage, the unexplained source, the distinction between a match and proof. His explicit denial ended up outside the frame of the story.

The page offered a button to share the discovery.

Cade stood at the crossing while the light changed.

Every word in the clip was his.
